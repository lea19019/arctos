### The Mythos of Model Interpretability

**File:** `Lipton-2018-Mythos-of-Model-Interpretability.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Zachary C. Lipton, 2016/2017  
**Venue:** 2016 ICML Workshop on Human Interpretability in Machine Learning (WHI); arXiv:1606.03490v3  
**Type:** paper (workshop/position paper)

> "Interpretability" is not one thing but a bundle of distinct, sometimes conflicting desiderata (trust, causality, transferability, informativeness, fairness) and properties (transparency vs. post-hoc explanation), so bare claims that a model "is" or "isn't" interpretable are meaningless without specifying which notion is meant.

**Problem:** Motivated by the common but under-examined claim that linear models are more interpretable than deep neural networks, Lipton asks: what does "interpretability" actually mean, and why do we want it? He observes that ML papers invoke the term axiomatically without a shared definition, giving the discourse a "quasi-scientific" character.

**Method:** This is a conceptual/taxonomic position paper, not an empirical one — no experiments, datasets, or metrics. Lipton surveys the interpretability literature and organizes it into two parts: (1) desiderata — the real-world goals people want interpretability to serve (trust, causality, transferability/robustness, informativeness for human decision-makers, fair/ethical/contestable decision-making); and (2) properties/techniques thought to confer interpretability, split into transparency (how the model works — simulatability: can a human step through the whole model in reasonable time; decomposability: does each input/parameter/calculation have an intuitive meaning; algorithmic transparency: do we understand the training procedure's convergence properties) versus post-hoc explanation (what else the model can tell us after training, without altering it — text explanations, visualizations like t-SNE and activation maximization/"Inceptionism," local explanations like saliency maps and LIME, and explanation-by-example via nearest neighbors in learned representation space, e.g. word2vec).

**Findings:** Key claims: (1) linear models are not strictly more interpretable than deep nets — linear models lose simulatability when high-dimensional and lose decomposability when built on heavily hand-engineered features, while deep nets often operate on raw/lightly-processed inputs and support rich post-hoc interpretation (visualization, explanation-by-example) that linear models don't as naturally afford. (2) Transparency and post-hoc explanation are genuinely different, sometimes competing, notions — e.g., saliency maps and LIME give post-hoc local explanations for black-box models without revealing the actual decision mechanism, and post-hoc explanations (like human verbal justifications) can be plausible yet unfaithful/misleading. (3) Any interpretability claim must specify which notion (simulatability, decomposability, algorithmic transparency, or a specific post-hoc method with a stated objective) it satisfies. (4) Demands for transparency can conflict with the broader goal of maximizing predictive performance/AI capability.

**Relevance:** This paper predates mechanistic interpretability as a subfield but supplies its conceptual vocabulary and cautionary framing: it draws exactly the transparency-vs-post-hoc distinction that mech interp work must locate itself within (mech interp aims at genuine mechanistic/algorithmic transparency, not merely plausible post-hoc stories), and its warning that post-hoc explanations can be fluent but unfaithful is directly relevant to evaluating claims about shared "interlingua" representations in multilingual models — one must ask whether evidence for a language-neutral representation (e.g., probing, activation patching, shared neurons) demonstrates real mechanistic structure or merely a plausible-looking post-hoc story.

### A Primer on the Inner Workings of Transformer-based Language Models

**File:** `Ferrando-2025-Primer-Inner-Workings-of-Transformer-LMs.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Ferrando, Sarti, Bisazza, Costa-jussà, 2024 (arXiv, v3 Oct 2024)  
**Type:** survey

> A unified, technical survey of interpretability methods for decoder-only Transformer LMs, organized around localizing which components drive a prediction and decoding what information those components encode, culminating in a catalog of known internal mechanisms.

**Problem:** Interpretability research on Transformer LMs has grown rapidly but is fragmented across notations and subfields (attention analysis, probing, causal interventions, sparse autoencoders, etc.), making it hard to see how findings connect or to get oriented on what is actually known about models' internal computation.

**Method:** The paper first establishes a unified notation for the Transformer forward pass using the "residual stream" view (Elhage et al.): attention OV/QK circuits, FFN key-value memory decomposition, and the linear decomposition of logits into per-component contributions. It then organizes interpretability techniques into two axes: (1) Behavior Localization — input attribution (gradient-based, perturbation-based, context-mixing) and model component attribution (direct logit attribution, causal/activation patching, path patching, circuit discovery via ACDC/EAP); and (2) Information Decoding — probing classifiers, the linear representation hypothesis with erasure/steering interventions, sparse autoencoders (SAE variants: Gated, TopK, JumpReLU) for disentangling superposition, and vocabulary-space decoding (logit lens, tuned lens, patchscopes, SVD/logit spectroscopy).

**Findings:** Section 5 catalogs discovered mechanisms: attention heads with interpretable roles (previous-token, induction, copy-suppression, successor heads), attention sinks and their "dark subspace" origin, FFN neurons (knowledge neurons, n-gram detectors, polysemanticity/superposition, universal neurons across model seeds), residual stream properties (exponential norm growth, outlier/rogue dimensions, memory management), and emergent multi-component circuits (e.g., the Indirect Object Identification circuit in GPT2-Small, factual recall circuits distinguishing subject-enrichment vs. attribute-extraction, grokking as circuit emergence). It closes with an overview of open-source interpretability tooling (TransformerLens, NNsight, SAELens, Inseq, etc.) and future directions toward human-interpretable, actionable explanations.

**Relevance:** Directly relevant as foundational vocabulary and methodology for a mech-interp/multilingual seminar: it formalizes the residual stream, circuits, and SAE toolkits used to test claims like the interlingua hypothesis, and explicitly discusses multilingual/cross-lingual findings (e.g., Ferrando & Costa-jussà 2024 showing identical subject-verb agreement circuits across English and Spanish, language-specific neurons controlling generation language, and induction heads performing fuzzy translation-like copying A*B*...A→B across languages) — concrete evidence points for whether multilingual models share language-neutral internal circuitry/representations.

### Towards A Rigorous Science of Interpretable Machine Learning

**File:** `DoshiVelez-Kim-2017-Rigorous-Science-of-Interpretable-ML.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Doshi-Velez and Kim, 2017  
**Type:** paper

> A position/framework paper arguing that "interpretability" in ML must be decomposed into a taxonomy of evaluation types (application-, human-, and functionally-grounded) plus a shared vocabulary of task- and method-related factors, rather than treated as a single vague, self-evident property.

**Problem:** There was no rigorous, agreed-upon definition or evaluation methodology for "interpretability" in ML circa 2017. Researchers either (1) claimed a system was interpretable because it worked well on some application, or (2) claimed a model class (sparse linear models, decision trees, rule lists) was inherently interpretable and optimized proxies like sparsity within it. Both approaches rest on "you'll know it when you see it" intuition, which cannot answer basic comparability questions: are two differently-interpretable models comparable? Do all applications have the same interpretability needs? The authors trace this need for interpretability to "incompleteness" in problem formalization (as opposed to mere statistical uncertainty) — i.e., cases where the objective function, safety constraints, or fairness criteria cannot be fully specified or enumerated in advance (scientific understanding, safety, ethics/fairness, mismatched objectives, multi-objective tradeoffs).

**Method:** The paper is conceptual/taxonomic rather than empirical. It defines interpretability as "the ability to explain or present in understandable terms to a human," then proposes a three-tier taxonomy of evaluation, increasing in specificity/cost: (1) Application-grounded evaluation — real humans (domain experts) doing the real end-task (e.g., doctors evaluated on diagnosis quality); (2) Human-grounded evaluation — real humans (lay subjects) doing simplified proxy tasks that preserve the essence of the application, using tests like binary forced choice between explanations, forward simulation/prediction (can a human predict the model's output from the explanation — exemplified by Chang et al.'s topic-model "intrusion detection" test), and counterfactual simulation (what input change would flip the output); (3) Functionally-grounded evaluation — no human subjects, using a proxy already validated by prior human studies (e.g., sparsity of a decision tree) to compare methods computationally. The paper then proposes a data-driven research program: build a matrix of (real-world tasks) x (interpretability methods) with performance as entries, and use matrix factorization to discover latent "factors of interpretability," analogous to collaborative filtering.

**Findings:** No empirical results — the paper's contribution is the taxonomy itself plus two sets of hypothesized latent dimensions. Task-related factors: global vs. local interpretability (patterns-in-general vs. reason-for-one-decision), area/severity of incompleteness, time constraints on the user, and nature of user expertise (domain experts vs. lay users, affecting "cognitive chunk" organization). Method-related factors: form of cognitive chunks (raw features, semantic derived features, prototypes), number of cognitive chunks, level of compositionality (rules/hierarchies), monotonicity/interaction structure among chunks, and how well humans handle uncertainty/stochasticity in explanations. The conclusion recommends that any interpretability paper explicitly state which evaluation tier it targets and which task/method factors it addresses, to avoid mismatched claims (e.g., citing sparsity metrics as evidence for a claim about real-world usability).

**Relevance:** Indirect but foundational relevance to a mech-interp/interlingua seminar: this paper supplies the methodological vocabulary for justifying *why* any interpretability claim (including "models learn a shared interlingual representation") needs to specify its evaluation grounding. A claim that a multilingual model's internal representations are "interpretable" as a shared interlingua should be evaluated at the appropriate tier — e.g., functionally-grounded probes/metrics (representation similarity across languages) versus human-grounded tasks (can a human predict cross-lingual behavior from the shared representation) versus application-grounded tests (does exploiting the shared representation improve real translation/transfer tasks). It's useful as a critical framework for the class to ask "what evidence tier is the interlingua hypothesis actually being tested at?" rather than a paper about multilingual models or mechanistic interpretability techniques directly.

### A Multiscale Visualization of Attention in the Transformer Model

**File:** `Vig-2019-Multiscale-Visualization-of-Attention.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Jesse Vig, 2019  
**Type:** paper

> Introduces BertViz, an open-source tool that visualizes Transformer attention at three nested scales (individual head, full model, individual neuron) to make the 384 attention structures of a model like BERT-Large tractable for interpretation.

**Problem:** Transformer models (BERT, GPT-2) use multi-layer, multi-head self-attention (e.g., BERT-Large has 24 layers x 16 heads = 384 distinct attention structures per input), which is very hard for a human to inspect and interpret directly. Prior visualization tools (attention-matrix heatmaps, bipartite graphs, Jones' 2017 tensor2tensor visualizer) only worked at a single scale and only for the original encoder-decoder Transformer.

**Method:** The paper extends Jones (2017)'s bipartite-graph attention visualization and adapts it from encoder-decoder to encoder-only (BERT) and decoder-only (GPT-2) architectures, adding two new views on top of the original one. (1) Attention-head view: lines connect attending tokens (left) to attended tokens (right), colored by head, weighted by attention score; supports filtering by token and, for BERT, by sentence-pair direction (Sentence A -> Sentence B). (2) Model view: a grid (layers x heads) of small-multiple thumbnails giving a birds-eye view of all attention patterns at once (following Tufte's "small multiples" design), clickable to expand into the head view. (3) Neuron view: decomposes the scaled dot-product attention computation itself, αi = softmax((qi·k1)/√d, ..., (qi·kN)/√d), showing per-neuron columns for Query q, Key k, their element-wise product q×k, the dot product q·k, and the final softmax — letting users trace which individual query/key neurons drive a given attention pattern. Demonstrated on pretrained GPT-2 small and BERT-base (uncased); code at github.com/jessevig/bertviz.

**Findings:** Using the tool, the author reports three qualitative use cases rather than benchmark numbers: (1) Bias detection — a GPT-2 attention head performs coreference-like resolution in a gendered way: "She" attends strongly to "nurse" and "He" attends more to "doctor" in the sentence "The doctor asked the nurse a question," and greedy-decoded continuations reflect this same gender association, suggesting encoded gender bias. (2) Locating relevant heads — in BERT's model view, heads useful for paraphrase/entailment tasks are visually identifiable by a distinctive "cross-hatch" thumbnail shape indicating cross-sentence (Sentence A -> Sentence B) attention (e.g., layer 3, head 0). (3) Linking neurons to behavior — in a GPT-2 head with distance-decaying attention (a soft, non-fixed-cutoff context window), the neuron view shows query vectors are nearly content-independent while a small subset of neuron positions in q×k monotonically decrease with token distance, i.e., specific neurons causally underlie the decay pattern, suggesting these could be edited to control effective context-window length. General observation: many heads also encode positional patterns (e.g., attend-to-previous-word), lexical patterns (list items, verbs, acronyms, named entities, paired punctuation, subject-verb pairs), and attention to the first token that likely reflects "null attention" (citing Vig and Belinkov, 2019).

**Relevance:** This is a foundational interpretability-tooling paper (predecessor/basis for the widely-used BertViz library) directly relevant to mechanistic interpretability methodology: it establishes attention-head-level and neuron-level visualization as a diagnostic technique and demonstrates that specific attention heads/neurons can be causally linked to model behaviors (gender bias, positional decay), foreshadowing circuit-style analysis. For the interlingua hypothesis specifically, the paper itself is monolingual (English BERT/GPT-2) and does not test cross-lingual representations, but its multiscale visualization methodology (head view, model view, neuron view) is a natural toolkit a seminar might apply to multilingual models to probe whether specific heads/neurons implement language-agnostic (shared) attention patterns across languages versus language-specific ones.

### What Does BERT Look At? An Analysis of BERT's Attention

**File:** `Clark-2019-What-Does-BERT-Look-At.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Clark, Khandelwal, Levy, and Manning, 2019  
**Type:** paper

> By analyzing BERT-base's 144 attention heads directly (rather than probing hidden states), the authors show that syntax-sensitive and coreference-sensitive attention patterns emerge purely from self-supervised masked-LM pre-training, with no explicit syntactic supervision.

**Problem:** Prior interpretability work on pre-trained language models focused either on model outputs (e.g., surprisal on targeted sentences) or on probing internal vector representations with trained classifiers. The authors ask instead what can be learned by directly examining attention weights — the naturally interpretable α_ij weights that determine how much each token contributes to another token's next-layer representation — in BERT-base (12 layers × 12 heads = 144 heads).

**Method:** Using BERT-base, they run several analyses: (1) surface-level statistics over 1000 Wikipedia segments — relative-position attention (previous/next token), attention to special tokens ([CLS], [SEP], periods/commas), and attention entropy per head/layer; (2) a "no-training-required" per-head classifier — for each word, the most-attended-to other word is treated as a prediction, evaluated against WSJ Penn Treebank (Stanford Dependencies) for syntactic relations (comparing against fixed-offset baselines) and against CoNLL-2012 for coreference antecedent selection (comparing against nearest-mention, head-match, and Lee et al. 2011 rule-based sieve baselines); (3) two novel attention-based probing classifiers (Attention-Only and Attention-and-Words, the latter combining attention weights with fixed GloVe embeddings via a bilinear scoring function) trained on top of frozen BERT attention maps to predict dependency parse trees, evaluated via UAS on the Penn Treebank; (4) a head-clustering analysis using Jensen-Shannon divergence between attention distributions, visualized via multidimensional scaling (MDS).

They also apply gradient-based feature attribution (Sundararajan et al. 2017, integrated-gradients-style) to test whether attention to [SEP] causally matters to the masked-LM loss.

Explicit finding cited: attention gradients w.r.t. [SEP] shrink starting at layer 5 (the same layer where [SEP] attention becomes dominant), suggesting attending to [SEP] is a low-impact "no-op" the model uses when a head's specialized function doesn't apply to the current token.

**Findings:** No single head captures syntax "overall" (best head: 34.5 UAS vs. 26.3 for a right-branching baseline), but individual heads specialize remarkably well to specific dependency relations: head 8-11 gets 94.3% accuracy on determiners (det), head 8-10 gets 86.8% on direct objects (dobj), head 9-6 gets 76.3% on prepositional objects (pobj), head 7-6 gets 80.5% on possessives (poss), and head 4-10 gets 82.5% on passive auxiliaries (auxpass) — all substantially beating fixed-offset baselines. Head 5-4 achieves 65% antecedent-selection accuracy on CoNLL-2012 coreference, beating string-matching (52%) and approaching a rule-based sieve system (69%). The combined Attention-and-Words probing classifier (attention weights + GloVe embeddings) reaches 77 UAS on dependency parsing, comparable to Hewitt & Manning's (2019) structural probe on hidden states (80 UUAS), suggesting attention maps carry roughly as much syntactic information as vector representations. Separately: over half of attention in layers 6-10 focuses on [SEP] (used as a no-op, not an aggregation point, per the gradient analysis); heads in the same layer cluster together by JS-divergence/MDS, an unexpectedly high degree of redundancy within layers.

**Relevance:** Directly relevant as a foundational method paper for the seminar's mechanistic-interpretability toolkit: it establishes attention-weight analysis (no-training-required "attention-as-classifier" probing, gradient-based attention attribution, and attention-based probing classifiers) as a complementary lens to hidden-state probing for locating linguistic structure inside a transformer. For the interlingua hypothesis specifically, this paper is monolingual (English-only, BERT-base) and doesn't address cross-lingual sharing, but its core methodology — showing specific heads specialize to specific relational/structural functions via self-supervision alone — is the template later work on multilingual models uses to ask whether analogous heads/circuits are shared or language-neutral across languages, and its finding that structure emerges without explicit supervision is the base case the interlingua literature extends to the cross-lingual setting.

### Contextual and Non-Contextual Word Embeddings: an in-depth Linguistic Investigation

**File:** `Miaschi-2020-Contextual-and-NonContextual-Embeddings.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Alessio Miaschi and Felice Dell'Orletta (2020)  
**Venue:** RepL4NLP 2020 (5th Workshop on Representation Learning for NLP, ACL Workshop, pages 110-119)  
**Type:** paper

> Using 68 linguistic probing tasks on English Universal Dependencies data, the paper shows that BERT's aggregated sentence representations encode roughly the same amount of implicit linguistic knowledge as simple non-contextual Word2vec sentence embeddings, and that BERT's single last-token embedding alone can match or beat full sentence representations.

**Problem:** Prior probing work established that contextual models like BERT encode rich linguistic structure, but few studies directly compared how much implicit linguistic knowledge a contextual model (BERT) versus a non-contextual model (Word2vec) actually packs into sentence-level representations, or asked whether BERT's contextual, sentence-spanning knowledge is also recoverable from a single word's embedding rather than requiring pooled/aggregated representations.

**Method:** The authors build a suite of 68 probing tasks (following Conneau et al. 2018's methodology, features drawn from Brunato et al. 2020) spanning raw-text (sentence length, word length, type/token ratio), morphosyntactic (POS distributions, verb inflection), and syntactic (parse tree depth, dependency link lengths, subordination, argument order, etc.) properties, evaluated on 23,943 sentences from three English UD treebanks (ParTUT, GUM, EWT). They extract layer-wise embeddings from BERT-base-uncased (12 layers) and from a self-trained 300-dim Word2vec (English Wikipedia dump, March 2020), combine word vectors into sentence vectors via four pooling strategies (Max, Min, Mean, Sum) plus BERT's [CLS] token, and train a Linear SVR probe per feature, evaluated with 5-fold cross-validation using Spearman's rho against a sentence-length correlation baseline. A second experiment repeats the probing using single-word embeddings only (first, last, and two internal tokens) instead of pooled sentence vectors, to test whether sentence-level properties survive in individual token representations.

**Findings:** Both models beat the length-correlation baseline, but averaged across all 68 features Word2vec sentence embeddings (best via Sum pooling, rho=0.60) slightly outperform BERT's averaged-layer representations (best via Mean pooling); BERT is stronger on raw-text and syntactic features while Word2vec is stronger on morphosyntactic (POS distribution) features, and BERT's advantage over Word2vec on syntax is concentrated in its lower/middle layers while it loses ground near the output layer. Strikingly, even a single BERT word embedding (especially the last token of the sentence, not the [CLS] token) captures a wide range of sentence-level syntactic/morphosyntactic properties, sometimes matching or exceeding full pooled sentence representations, while single Word2vec tokens perform far worse (no contextual information to draw on) — suggesting BERT compresses whole-sentence structural information into individual contextual token vectors.

**Relevance:** This is a monolingual (English-only) probing-methodology paper, not a multilingual/interlingua study, but it is directly useful groundwork for the mechanistic-interpretability/interlingua discussion because it establishes the probing-task toolkit, pooling-strategy choices, and the key finding that contextual embeddings can concentrate sentence-level linguistic structure into single-token representations — a methodological template that multilingual-interlingua papers (e.g., probing mBERT/XLM-R layers across languages) build on when asking whether shared cross-lingual representations encode language-neutral syntactic structure in the same way.

### BertViz: A Tool for Visualizing Multi-Head Self-Attention in the BERT Model

**File:** `Vig-2019-BertViz-Tool.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Jesse Vig, 2019  
**Type:** paper

> BertViz is an open-source interactive visualization tool that exposes BERT's multi-head self-attention at three levels of granularity (head, model, neuron), enabling interpretation and debugging of what attention patterns the model learns and how it computes them.

**Problem:** Attention weights in Transformer models like BERT are a potential window into model behavior, but existing visualization tools were either generic heatmaps or were built for encoder-decoder Transformers (e.g., the Tensor2Tensor visualizer by Jones 2017), not tailored to BERT's encoder-only, bidirectional, sentence-pair architecture. There was no tool that let practitioners inspect attention at multiple levels of granularity — from individual heads down to the underlying query/key neuron computations — or use that inspection to debug concrete model errors.

**Method:** The paper adapts and extends the Tensor2Tensor attention visualizer for BERT_BASE, adding two new views on top of the existing head-level view: (1) Attention-head view — bipartite line graphs connecting attending tokens (left) to attended tokens (right) within a chosen layer/head, with line weight = attention score, color = head identity, and filters for sentence-A/B directionality and per-token attention; (2) Model view — a small-multiples grid (Tufte's design pattern) of all layer x head thumbnail attention patterns, letting users scan how attention evolves across all 12 layers x 12 heads at once; (3) Neuron view — for a single selected token, traces the actual computation of attention head-by-head through columns showing the 64-dim query vector, each token's 64-dim key vector, their element-wise product (q x k), the resulting dot product (q.k), and the final softmax attention weight, with blue/orange coloring for positive/negative values. The tool is demonstrated qualitatively (no quantitative evaluation) on toy sentences ("the cat sat on the mat" / "the cat lay on the rug") and on a debugging case study using BERT_BASE fine-tuned on the MRPC paraphrase corpus (Dolan & Brockett, 2005).

**Findings:** Using the model view, the authors observe that early layers (0-2) capture low-level positional patterns (e.g., attention to the next token), while late layers (9-11) focus heavily on sentence-separator tokens ([SEP]), suggesting these encode higher-level, sentence-level representations. The neuron view reveals that for a within-sentence attention head, only 3-4 query/key neuron dimensions dominate the dot product, and these neurons have same-sign high-magnitude values for same-sentence token pairs but opposite-sign values for cross-sentence pairs — mechanistically explaining the observed attention pattern. In the debugging case study, the tool is used to diagnose why a fine-tuned BERT paraphrase-detection model failed to link "awesome" and "sick" as a paraphrase pair: the attention-head view showed no attention edge between the two words in a head that otherwise captures cross-sentence relatedness, and substituting synonyms (terrific vs. an unrelated word available) confirmed the model's attention pattern tracked its (in)correct predictions.

**Relevance:** This is a foundational interpretability-tooling paper rather than a study of multilingual representations or the interlingua hypothesis directly, but it's methodologically relevant: it establishes attention visualization at head/model/neuron granularity as a standard technique for probing what individual attention heads compute and how, which is exactly the kind of tool a student would reach for to inspect whether specific attention heads in a multilingual model behave language-agnostically (e.g., attending to semantically corresponding tokens across languages) versus language-specific. The neuron-level decomposition (q x k contribution per dimension) is a precursor to later mechanistic techniques for localizing which components carry language-neutral vs. language-specific information.

### What you can cram into a single $&!#* vector: Probing sentence embeddings for linguistic properties

**File:** `Conneau-2018-What-You-Can-Cram-Into-a-Single-Vector.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Conneau, Kruszewski, Lample, Barrault, Baroni, 2018  
**Type:** paper

> Introduces 10 "probing tasks" — simple, single-sentence-embedding classification problems isolating surface, syntactic, and semantic properties — to systematically test what linguistic information different sentence encoders and training objectives actually encode.

**Problem:** Sentence embeddings are usually evaluated via complex downstream tasks (sentiment classification, entailment, etc.), which makes it hard to pinpoint what linguistic information a representation actually contains, since success could stem from spurious heuristics or hidden biases rather than genuine linguistic knowledge. The paper asks: what specific properties (surface, syntactic, semantic) do sentence embeddings actually preserve, and how does this depend on encoder architecture vs. training objective?

**Method:** The authors design 10 probing tasks grouped into three categories: (1) Surface — SentLen (6-way sentence-length bucket classification) and WC/word content (1000-way: which of 1000 mid-frequency words appears in the sentence); (2) Syntactic — BShift (detect an adjacent-word swap), TreeDepth (8-way classification of parse-tree depth, decorrelated from length), TopConst (20-way classification of the sequence of top constituents under the S node); (3) Semantic — Tense, SubjNum, ObjNum (tense/number of main clause verb/subject/object, with no lexical overlap between train/test), SOMO (detect a noun/verb swapped for a frequency-matched distractor), and CoordInv (detect inverted order of two coordinate clauses). All data is drawn from the Toronto Book Corpus (parsed with the Stanford PCFG parser), with 100k train / 10k dev / 10k test balanced examples per task. They probe embeddings from three encoder architectures — BiLSTM-last, BiLSTM-max, and a gated ConvNet — each trained (or left untrained/random) under eight objectives: NMT (En-Fr, En-De, En-Fi via Europarl), autoencoding, Seq2Tree (parsing as seq2seq), SkipThought, and Natural Language Inference (SNLI+MultiNLI), plus untrained baselines. A frozen sentence embedding feeds an MLP classifier per probing task (no fine-tuning), and they compare against Naive Bayes tf-idf, sentence-length, and bag-of-vectors (BoV-fastText) baselines plus human-annotated upper bounds. They also correlate probing-task scores with 10+ downstream SentEval tasks (Spearman correlation).

**Findings:** Key results: (1) Bag-of-vectors baselines are surprisingly strong on Tense/SubjNum/ObjNum/TopConst due to natural redundancy in language (most verbs/nouns in a sentence share the target tense/number), showing word-embedding aggregation alone captures a lot. (2) Trained encoders substantially outperform BoV and baselines but remain well below human upper bounds on TreeDepth, BShift, SOMO, and CoordInv — the tasks requiring deeper syntactic/semantic/pragmatic structure. (3) Architecture matters as much as training objective: BiLSTM-max, BiLSTM-last, and GatedConvNet trained identically produce linguistically distinct embeddings; BiLSTM-max consistently beats BiLSTM-last (except on SentLen) and untrained BiLSTM-max already performs remarkably well, implying architectural priors bias what gets encoded before any training. (4) NMT training yields more linguistically-rich embeddings than NLI training (better on most probing tasks), even though NLI training is known to be better for downstream SentEval performance — suggesting downstream tasks reward shallower/word-content features (WC correlates positively with nearly all downstream tasks) while abstracting away surface features like sentence length (SentLen negatively correlates with downstream performance). (5) Seq2Tree performance on syntax tasks exceeds the human bound because it mimics the same parser's errors, an artifact rather than genuine superiority.

**Relevance:** This is a foundational interpretability/probing-methodology paper (not multilingual per se) that establishes the "probing classifier" paradigm widely reused in multilingual interpretability work, including studies of the interlingua hypothesis: training a simple classifier on frozen representations to test whether specific structured information (here linguistic properties; in multilingual work, language identity vs. language-neutral content) is linearly/non-linearly decodable. Students should note the methodological cautions here — controlling for lexical overlap, redundancy confounds, and baseline comparisons — as these are exactly the pitfalls that plague naive probing studies of shared multilingual representations, and the finding that architecture/training-objective choices strongly precondition what gets encoded is directly relevant to arguments about why some multilingual models induce shared interlingua-like spaces and others don't.

### What You Can Cram Into a Single #$!@* Vector: Probing Sentence Embeddings for Linguistic Properties

**File:** `Conneau-2018-Cram-Single-Vector-SLIDES.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Conneau, Kruszewski, Lample, Barrault, Baroni, 2018  
**Type:** slide deck

> Introduces a suite of 10 "probing tasks" — simple classification tasks isolating a single surface/syntactic/semantic property each — to determine what linguistic information fixed-size sentence embeddings actually encode, independent of downstream task performance.

**Problem:** Universal sentence embeddings (single fixed-length vectors) are typically evaluated only via transfer/downstream performance (SentEval tasks like sentiment classification, NLI, STS), which is too holistic and confounded to reveal what specific linguistic properties an encoder actually captures. The paper asks: what information — surface, syntactic, semantic — can actually be "crammed" into a single sentence vector, and how does this vary with encoder architecture and training objective?

**Method:** The authors define 10 binary/multi-class probing tasks built from natural sentences (5-28 words) drawn from the Toronto Book Corpus (TBC), each with 100k train / 10k valid / 10k test balanced examples, with obvious lexical biases removed. Tasks span three categories: (1) Surface — SentLen (6-way sentence length bin), WC/Word Content (which of 1000 candidate words appears in the sentence); (2) Syntactic — TopConst (20-way top constituent parse-tree sequence, via Stanford parser), BShift/Bigram Shift (was a bigram swapped), TreeDepth, Tense (past/present of main clause), SubjNum/ObjNum (singular/plural of subject/object); (3) Semantic — SOMO/Semantic Odd Man Out (was a noun/verb replaced by another of the same POS), CoordInv/Coordination Inversion (were two coordinate clauses swapped). A simple MLP classifier is trained on top of frozen sentence embeddings for each probing task (no fine-tuning of the encoder). They evaluate ~30 encoders crossing 3 architectures (BiLSTM-last, BiLSTM-max, Gated ConvNet) with 7 training objectives (AutoEncoder, Seq2Tree, SkipThought, NLI/InferSent-style, and Seq2seq NMT without attention for En-Fr, En-De, En-Fi), plus baselines (human evaluation, length-only, Naive Bayes with uni/bi-gram TF-IDF, CBOW/averaged word embeddings, majority vote).

Note on the reading: this is a slide deck (28 slides), fully read in one pass — no truncation occurred, so this covers the complete presented content (motivation, task definitions, experimental design, and all reported results/takeaways).

**Findings:** Different training objectives induce very different encoded properties: NMT-trained encoders and Seq2Tree do well on syntactic tasks (TopConst, ObjNum) but Seq2Tree destroys word content (WC) information (14% vs CBOW's 91.6%), while AutoEncoder preserves SentLen almost perfectly (99.3%) but is weak on WC (23.3%). CBOW (bag of averaged word vectors) is surprisingly strong on WC (91.6%) since it directly retains lexical identity, but far weaker on syntactic/order-sensitive tasks (BShift near chance). Architecture matters less than training objective, though BiLSTM-max and Gated ConvNet generally beat BiLSTM-last, especially on BShift (word-order sensitivity: 72-73% vs 62.4%). Tracking probing accuracy across training epochs shows most properties are acquired early and plateau, and for NMT training, Word Content accuracy tracks BLEU score, while SentLen accuracy actually decreases as translation quality improves (the model discards raw length info in favor of more abstract structure). Critically, correlation analysis between probing scores and SentEval downstream task performance (MR, CR, SUBJ, MPQA, SST2/5, TREC, MRPC, SICK-E/R, STSB) shows Word Content is by far the strongest and most consistent positive predictor of downstream performance across nearly all tasks, while syntactic/semantic probing scores correlate much more weakly or inconsistently — suggesting current downstream benchmarks may be dominated by lexical/surface cues rather than deeper structural understanding. The overarching takeaway: sentence embeddings need not score well on probing tasks per se — the tasks are diagnostic tools for comparing what different encoders capture, not a target to optimize. Probing task data and code were released via SentEval on GitHub.

**Relevance:** This is a foundational probing-classifier methodology paper for interpretability of distributed representations: training a lightweight auxiliary classifier on frozen embeddings to test for encoded properties is exactly the "probing" paradigm later extended to multilingual/interlingua work (e.g., probing whether multilingual models encode language-neutral syntactic/semantic structure in a shared subspace). The task taxonomy (surface vs. syntactic vs. semantic) and the finding that different training objectives yield qualitatively different "cram"-ed information directly inform how one would design analogous probes for testing the interlingua hypothesis — e.g., probing whether a shared representation across languages still preserves syntactic structure or discards language-specific surface form, mirroring this paper's SentLen/WC vs. TopConst/BShift contrasts.

### BERT Rediscovers the Classical NLP Pipeline

**File:** `Tenney-2019-BERT-Rediscovers-Classical-NLP-Pipeline.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Tenney, Das, Pavlick, 2019  
**Type:** paper

> Using edge probing classifiers on frozen BERT layer activations, the paper shows BERT encodes a linear "pipeline" of NLP tasks (POS -> constituents -> dependencies -> semantic roles -> coreference) across depth, but individual sentences can revise earlier decisions using later, higher-level information.

**Problem:** Whether pre-trained deep encoders like BERT actually learn the kind of structured linguistic abstractions (syntax, semantics) traditionally assumed necessary for NLP, or merely model surface co-occurrence statistics, and specifically where in the network's depth such information is localized.

**Method:** The authors apply the "edge probing" methodology (Tenney et al. 2019) to frozen (not fine-tuned) BERT-base and BERT-large encoders: for each of 8 tasks (POS, constituents, dependencies, entities, SRL, coreference, semantic proto-roles/SPR, relation classification/SemEval), a lightweight probing classifier receives only the contextual vectors within one or two token spans and predicts a structured label, using standard datasets (OntoNotes 5.0, English Web Treebank, SPR1, SemEval 2010 Task 8) scored with micro-averaged F1. Two complementary metrics are computed per task: (1) scalar mixing weights, a learned ELMo-style softmax-weighted sum over layers (per Peters et al. 2018a Eq.1) whose "center of gravity" indicates which layers the probe finds most useful; (2) cumulative scoring, where classifiers P_τ^(ℓ) are trained with access to layers 0...ℓ, yielding a differential score Δτ^(ℓ) (marginal F1 gain per layer) and an "expected layer" statistic. KL divergence from uniform, K(·), measures how concentrated/localized each task's layer distribution is.

**Findings:** Both metrics show a consistent ordering across layers: POS tags resolved earliest, then constituents, then dependencies, then semantic roles, then coreference last — mirroring the classical NLP pipeline. Syntactic tasks (POS, constituents, dependencies) are highly localized (high K values, concentrated in early-mid layers), while semantic tasks (SPR, relation classification, entities) are diffusely spread across nearly all layers with weak localization, suggesting BERT does not expose a clean localized feature set for these. Differential scores peak early (layers 1-7 of BERT-large, via shortcut heuristics) while mixing weights concentrate later (layers 9-20), and the same relative ordering/"stretching effect" holds comparing BERT-base (12 layers) to BERT-large (24 layers). Qualitative per-example analysis (e.g. "he smoked toronto...", "china today blacked out...") shows the pipeline order holds only in aggregate: on individual sentences BERT can revise lower-level decisions (e.g., entity type of "Toronto" from GPE to ORG) after resolving higher-level structure (SRL role ARG1), demonstrating dynamic, non-monotonic use of hierarchical information.

**Relevance:** This is a foundational probing/localization paper for mechanistic interpretability methodology (edge probing, scalar mixing, cumulative scoring, layer-wise localization via KL-from-uniform) that a later multilingual "interlingua hypothesis" discussion would likely contrast with: this paper studies monolingual (English) task/layer localization, establishing tools and a within-language depth-wise organization story, whereas the interlingua question asks whether analogous shared, language-neutral abstractions/layers exist across languages rather than across task types within one language.

### What Do You Learn From Context? Probing for Sentence Structure in Contextualized Word Representations

**File:** `Tenney-2019-What-Do-You-Learn-From-Context.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Tenney, Xia, Chen, Wang, Poliak, McCoy, Kim, Van Durme, Bowman, Das, Pavlick, 2019  
**Type:** paper

> Introduces "edge probing" — a unified suite of 8 sub-sentence classification tasks (POS, constituents, dependencies, entities, SRL, coreference, SPR, relation classification) — to systematically test what syntactic vs. semantic structure ELMo, CoVe, GPT, and BERT encode at each token position.

**Problem:** Prior probing work looked mostly at isolated token-level properties (POS, morphology, word sense) or whole-sentence encodings. The authors want a uniform, fine-grained methodology to ask: what do contextual word representations capture about sentence structure — syntactic, semantic, local, or long-range — that static word embeddings do not?

**Method:** Edge probing formalizes each structured NLP task (POS tagging, constituent labeling, dependency labeling, named entity typing, semantic role labeling, coreference, semantic proto-role labeling, relation classification) as predicting a label over one or two given spans in a sentence, using gold span boundaries from datasets like OntoNotes 5.0, Universal Dependencies, SPR1/SPR2, Winograd/DPR, and SemEval 2010 Task 8. A lightweight probing classifier (projection layer + self-attentive span pooling of Lee et al. 2017 + 2-layer MLP + sigmoid, binary F1 metric) sits on top of frozen contextual encoders (CoVe, ELMo, GPT, BERT-base/large), so it can only use information already present in the fixed embeddings. They compare against lexical (context-free) baselines, a randomized/orthonormal-weight ELMo baseline (to isolate architecture from learned pretraining), and word-level CNN baselines with kernel width ±1/±2 (to isolate local vs. long-range context).

**Findings:** Contextual encoders give the largest gains over lexical baselines on syntactic tasks (constituents +15-25 F1, dependencies, SRL core roles) and much smaller gains on semantic tasks (SPR +1-5 F1, Winograd coreference often near zero for weaker models). BERT-large is best overall (macro F1 87.3, +2.9 over ELMo), with especially large gains on OntoNotes coreference (+7.4 F1) and Winograd coreference (+7.8 F1), suggesting deeper models help more on hard semantic tasks. Scalar mixing across layers outperforms using only the top layer, indicating relevant information lives in intermediate layers. The learned (pretrained) weights account for >70% of ELMo's improvement over the orthonormal/random baseline, but a CNN with only ±2 token context captures 79% of ELMo's gain on average — except on dependency labeling, where full ELMo maintains high F1 even at long token distances (only dropping ~7 F1 from distance 0 to 8), showing genuine long-range information is captured for some phenomena.

**Relevance:** This is a methodological ancestor for mechanistic interpretability of multilingual/interlingua questions: edge probing gives a template for asking "what linguistic structure is encoded where" via lightweight classifiers on frozen representations, which is directly transferable to probing whether multilingual models encode language-neutral syntactic/semantic structure at shared layers/positions. Its finding that syntax is captured much more robustly than semantics by self-supervised pretraining is a relevant prior for evaluating whether shared interlingua representations in multilingual models are more syntactic or semantic in nature.

### ERASER: A Benchmark to Evaluate Rationalized NLP Models

**File:** `DeYoung-2020-ERASER-Benchmark.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** DeYoung, Jain, Rajani, Lehman, Xiong, Socher, Wallace, 2020  
**Type:** paper

> A benchmark that repurposes seven existing NLP datasets with human-annotated "rationales" (evidence spans) and proposes standardized metrics for measuring both agreement-with-humans and faithfulness of model-extracted rationales.

**Problem:** Interpretability research in NLP, specifically work on models that produce "rationales" (supporting text snippets) for their predictions, was being conducted on disparate datasets with idiosyncratic, non-comparable metrics, making it hard to track progress or compare methods across the field — analogous to the fragmentation GLUE/SuperGLUE fixed for general NLU.

**Method:** The authors assembled seven datasets — Evidence Inference, BoolQ, Movie Reviews, FEVER, MultiRC, CoS-E (CommonsenseQA), and e-SNLI — converting each into a unified format of (input, label, rationale). For several datasets they collected new "comprehensive" rationale annotations (marking ALL supporting evidence, not just sufficient snippets) on subsets of test data via Upwork/Mechanical Turk annotators, and report human inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's kappa, token-F1). They define two families of metrics: (1) agreement metrics comparing model-extracted rationales to human rationales — Intersection-Over-Union (IOU) F1 for discrete/hard-selection models, and AUPRC for continuous/soft-scoring models; and (2) faithfulness metrics — comprehensiveness (does removing the rationale reduce model confidence?) and sufficiency (can the rationale alone reproduce the prediction?), computed both directly and as an aggregate "Area Over the Perturbation Curve" (AOPC) across token-importance bins. They benchmark baseline architectures: hard-selection models (Lei et al. 2016's REINFORCE-trained extract-then-predict model with BERT/GloVe+LSTM encoders; pipeline models with independent rationale-identification and classification stages, including a "BERT-to-BERT" pipeline) and soft-selection models (BERT+LSTM with attention, gradient, or LIME-based token scoring).

**Findings:** No single off-the-shelf architecture handles all datasets well given their widely varying document/rationale lengths and granularities, motivating a need for length-adaptive rationale models. Training with explicit rationale supervision generally (not always) improves agreement with human rationales but doesn't strongly correlate with task accuracy. Attention-based token scoring achieves higher AUPRC (more human-plausible) but worse faithfulness (comprehensiveness/sufficiency) than gradient- or LIME-based scoring — corroborating prior "attention is not explanation" findings; LIME notably performs well on faithfulness. The paper stresses these are not intended as a definitive/settled leaderboard but a starting resource, since faithfulness measurement remains an open problem.

**Relevance:** Less directly about multilingual models or the interlingua hypothesis, but foundational for the seminar's broader mechanistic interpretability toolkit: it formalizes the plausibility-vs-faithfulness distinction (do explanations look right to humans vs. do they actually reflect what the model used) that is central to interpreting any model's internal reasoning, including cross-lingual/shared representations. Techniques like comprehensiveness/sufficiency perturbation metrics could be adapted to test whether hypothesized shared multilingual features are causally load-bearing, and the paper's caution against trusting attention as faithful is directly relevant to feature-attribution claims in multilingual interp work.

### Locating and Editing Factual Associations in GPT

**File:** `Meng-2022-Locating-and-Editing-Factual-Associations-ROME.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Meng, Bau, Andonian, and Belinkov, 2022  
**Venue:** NeurIPS 2022  
**Type:** paper

> Factual associations in autoregressive GPT models are stored as a localized, key-value-like computation in mid-layer MLP modules at the last subject token, and can be directly edited with a closed-form rank-one weight update (ROME).

**Problem:** The paper asks where and how autoregressive transformer LMs (GPT-2 XL, GPT-J) store factual associations of the form (subject, relation, object) — e.g., "The Space Needle is located in [Seattle]" — and whether that storage location can be verified by directly manipulating it, rather than just correlating internal representations with outputs (the usual limitation of probing classifiers).

**Method:** Two-stage approach. (1) Causal Tracing: run the model three times per prompt — clean, corrupted (subject token embeddings perturbed with Gaussian noise), and corrupted-with-restoration (a single hidden state at one layer/token is patched back to its clean value) — and measure the Average Indirect Effect (AIE) each state has on restoring the correct prediction, over a sample of 1000 factual statements. By further severing MLP or attention computation paths in a modified causal graph, they isolate MLP contributions from attention contributions. (2) Rank-One Model Editing (ROME): treats a single mid-layer MLP's down-projection matrix W_proj as a linear associative (key-value) memory (à la Kohonen/Anderson). Given a target fact, it computes a key vector k* (the MLP's post-nonlinearity activation at the subject's last token, averaged over random prefix texts) and optimizes a value vector v* via gradient descent to maximize the target object's probability while minimizing KL divergence from the original model's predictions on "{subject} is a" prompts (preserving subject "essence"). It then inserts (k*, v*) via a closed-form constrained least-squares rank-one weight update to W_proj, using a pre-cached covariance statistic C estimated from Wikipedia text. Evaluated against baselines: Fine-Tuning (FT), Constrained FT (FT+L), Knowledge Neurons (KN), and hypernetwork methods (KE, MEND) on zsRE and on a new COUNTERFACT dataset of deliberately low-probability counterfactual facts, scored on Efficacy, Generalization (paraphrase), Specificity (neighborhood), fluency, and consistency.

**Findings:** Causal Tracing reveals two causally important sites: a "late site" (last layer, last token, attention-dominated) and a novel "early site" — strongly causal individual states at middle layers (peak AIE ~8.7% at layer 15 in GPT-2 XL) specifically at the last token of the subject, dominated by MLP contributions (AIE 6.6% vs 1.6% for attention there). This supports a hypothesis that mid-layer MLPs recall subject properties which are then copied to the final token by later attention. ROME, despite being a single-layer rank-one edit, matches or beats FT/FT+L/KE/MEND on zsRE (99.8% efficacy) and dramatically outperforms all baselines on COUNTERFACT (Score 89.2 vs next-best 66.9 on GPT-2 XL; 91.5 vs 68.7 on GPT-J), where competitors systematically fail either at generalization (overfitting to the literal statement) or specificity (bleeding into unrelated "neighborhood" facts) — ROME achieves both simultaneously. Editing performance peaks exactly at the mid-layers and subject-last-token position identified by causal tracing, closing the loop between the correlational (tracing) and causal-intervention (editing) evidence. Limitations: edits one fact at a time, are directional (not symmetric), and edited models will still "guess" plausible but false facts.

**Relevance:** Directly relevant as a canonical mechanistic-interpretability method paper: it establishes causal (not just correlational) tools for localizing knowledge in transformer internals and demonstrates that factual knowledge is stored in a structured, editable, linear-algebraic form (key-value memory) at a specific, predictable circuit location. For the interlingua hypothesis, ROME/Causal Tracing is a template methodology students may extend to ask whether multilingual factual associations share a single language-neutral (s,r,o) representation/circuit across languages, or whether each language has its own localized MLP key-value store — i.e., whether "Seattle" as the answer for "Space Needle" is retrieved via the same mid-layer subject-token mechanism regardless of input language, which would be direct mechanistic evidence for (or against) a shared interlingua representation.

### Locating and Editing Factual Associations in GPT

**File:** `Meng-2022-ROME-SLIDES.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Kevin Meng, David Bau, Alex Andonian, Yonatan Belinkov, 2022  
**Type:** slide deck

> GPT-style autoregressive transformers store individual factual associations (subject-relation-object) in a small set of mid-layer MLP modules, which can be causally located via activation patching and then directly edited with a closed-form rank-one weight update (ROME).

**Problem:** Where inside a large language model is a specific fact like "The Space Needle is located in Seattle" stored, and can that localized knowledge be surgically edited (e.g., changed to "Rome") without retraining the whole model or damaging unrelated knowledge?

**Method:** Three-part approach: (i) Causal Tracing — run the model on a factual prompt, corrupt the subject tokens' embeddings (adding noise, "*"), then selectively restore individual hidden states (or windows of MLP/attention outputs) from the clean run one at a time to measure the "Average Indirect Effect" (AIE) on the correct answer's probability, localizing which layer/token/module causally mediates the prediction. This is done on GPT-2-XL and GPT-J. (ii) ROME (Rank-One Model Editing) — treats an MLP layer's weight matrix W as a key-value associative memory (key = subject representation from W_fc, value = fact representation fed to W_proj, following Geva et al. 2021 / Dai et al. 2021). Under the assumption that pretrained weights solve least-squares recall (W0 KK^T = VK^T), inserting a new key-value pair (k*, v*) while minimizing disruption to existing associations is solved as constrained least squares, yielding a closed-form rank-one update: W1 = W0 + Λ(C⁻¹k*)^T, where C = KK^T is a (precomputed) second-moment/covariance statistic of prior keys. The new value vector v* is obtained by optimizing a hidden-state vector at the chosen MLP layer so the model outputs the target fact. (iii) CounterFact benchmark — a new dataset of 21,919 counterfactual edits, each with a subject-relation-object statement plus paraphrase prompts (test generalization), neighborhood prompts about related subjects (test specificity, i.e. no unwanted interference), and generation prompts (test whether edits propagate coherently into free text).

**Findings:** Causal tracing reveals a bimodal pattern: an "early site" at mid-layer MLPs on the last subject token has the largest causal effect (supporting the mid-layer-MLP-as-fact-storage hypothesis), and a separate "late site" driven by attention near the end of the sequence. On CounterFact, ROME achieves the best combined Efficacy, Generalization, and Specificity ("Score") on both GPT-2-XL (89.2) and GPT-J (91.5), clearly outperforming baselines — direct fine-tuning (FT, FT+L), hypernetwork editors (KE, MEND), and the interpretability-based Knowledge Neurons (KN) method — each of which fails badly on either generalization or specificity (e.g., FT gets ~90% of neighborhood/specificity prompts wrong on GPT-J). Human evaluation shows ROME edits are judged more consistent than GPT/FT+L outputs but somewhat less fluent. Noted limitation: edits are not inherently bidirectional (editing "Microsoft founded by Bill Gates" doesn't automatically update "Bill Gates, founder of Microsoft").

**Relevance:** This is a foundational mechanistic-interpretability paper for localizing and manipulating specific factual/conceptual content inside a model's weights via causal intervention rather than just probing activations — directly relevant methodology (causal tracing, key-value memory view of MLPs, rank-one edits) for testing the interlingua hypothesis, e.g. whether a single shared "fact slot" mediates recall across languages or whether each language has separate localized associations, and whether editing one language's fact representation transfers to other languages.

### Dissecting Recall of Factual Associations in Auto-Regressive Language Models

**File:** `Geva-2023-Dissecting-Recall-of-Factual-Associations.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Geva, Bastings, Filippova, Globerson (Google DeepMind/Tel Aviv Univ), 2023  
**Venue:** EMNLP 2023, pages 12216-12235  
**Type:** paper

> Using causal interventions and vocabulary projections on GPT-2/GPT-J, the paper shows factual recall (e.g. "Beats Music is owned by → Apple") proceeds in three stages: early MLPs enrich the last-subject-token representation with subject attributes, the relation propagates to the last token, and upper-layer attention heads then extract the specific attribute from the enriched subject representation using subject-attribute mappings stored in their own parameters.

**Problem:** Prior work (e.g. Meng et al. 2022, ROME) located WHERE factual knowledge is stored (mid-layer MLPs) but not HOW it is retrieved during inference for a given subject-relation query. This paper asks: given a query like "Beats Music is owned by ___", what internal computational steps let the model output "Apple"?

**Method:** Two methods, both reverse-engineering/causal-intervention style inspired by Wang et al. 2022 (IOI circuit): (1) Attention Knockout — zero out attention edges (M^{l,j}_rc = -inf) between two positions over a window of layers to test whether critical information flows between them, applied to edges from subject/relation/last positions into the final position; (2) Vocabulary projection / "logit lens" style analysis (following Dar et al. 2022, Geva et al. 2022, Nostalgebraist 2020) — project intermediate hidden states through the unembedding matrix to see what tokens they promote, plus a novel "attributes rate" metric (fraction of top-50 projected tokens that appear in a BM25-retrieved Wikipedia-derived candidate set for the subject). They also do sublayer knockout (zeroing MLP or MHSA outputs at the last-subject position for 10-layer windows) and inspect attention head parameter matrices (W_VO) projected into embedding space to find subject→attribute mappings directly encoded in weights. Data: COUNTERFACT dataset (Meng et al. 2022a), restricted to correctly-predicted queries (1,209 for GPT-2-XL [48 layers, 1.5B params], 1,199 for GPT-J [28 layers, 6B params]).

**Findings:** Two critical information-flow points into the last position: first from relation (non-subject) tokens in early-mid layers, then from subject tokens in mid-upper layers — blocking subject attention drops prediction probability by up to 60%. The last-subject-token representation's "attributes rate" rises through the layers to ~50%, driven overwhelmingly by early MLP sublayers (knocking out MLPs cuts attributes rate by ~88%; knocking out MHSA cuts it <30%), confirming MLPs as key-value memories enriching the subject representation — this happens in lower/early layers, not just the mid layers previously implicated by ROME. Static token embeddings alone contain far less attribute information (attributes rate 19.3/4.1 for GPT-2 individual/mean token embeddings vs ~50% at upper layers). Attribute extraction itself is performed mainly by upper MHSA sublayers (extraction/agreement rate 68.2% for GPT-2, 76.7% for GPT-J) rather than MLPs (31-41%); extraction depends causally on both the enriched subject and the last-position query (patching early subject reps cuts extraction rate up to 50%). Critically, for ~30% (GPT-2) / ~39% (GPT-J) of extraction events, a specific attention head's W_VO parameter matrix directly encodes the subject→attribute mapping when projected to vocabulary space — some heads act as "knowledge hubs" encoding hundreds of such mappings (e.g. a head mapping Finnish→Finland, Chicago→Illinois, etc.), challenging the common view that MLPs alone store extractable factual associations.

**Relevance:** Directly relevant to mechanistic interpretability methodology (attention knockout as causal ablation, logit-lens/vocabulary projection, weight-based circuit analysis via W_VO in embedding space) that a course on multilingual interpretability would build on. While this paper is monolingual (English factual recall only, no cross-lingual analysis), its findings about where/how factual associations are stored (early MLPs) versus extracted (upper attention heads with parameter-encoded subject-attribute mappings) form a natural baseline against which to test the interlingua hypothesis — e.g., whether the same three-stage mechanism (subject enrichment → relation propagation → attention-based extraction) operates on a shared, language-neutral representation across languages, or whether the enrichment/extraction stages are language-specific. Students should be primed to ask whether the "knowledge hub" attention heads found here encode language-neutral or language-specific subject-attribute mappings when the same experiment is run multilingually.

### Steering Language Models with Activation Engineering

**File:** `Turner-2023-Activation-Addition.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Turner, Thiergart, Leech, Udell, Vazquez, Mini, MacDiarmid, 2023 (v5 Oct 2024)  
**Type:** paper

> Introduces Activation Addition (ActAdd), a training-free method that computes a "steering vector" from the activation difference between two contrasting prompts (e.g. "Love" vs "Hate") and adds it into a transformer's residual stream at inference time to control high-level output properties like sentiment and toxicity.

**Problem:** LLMs have an "elicitation overhang": prompting and finetuning don't reliably surface everything a model is capable of. Prompt engineering is unreliable/discrete, and finetuning/RLHF/weight-editing are costly and have known side effects on general performance. The paper asks whether directly perturbing intermediate activations at inference time can steer model outputs more reliably and cheaply than these alternatives, without touching weights.

**Method:** ActAdd operates on the residual stream of a decoder-only transformer. Given a contrast pair of prompts (p+, p-) (e.g. "Love" / "Hate"), it right-pads them to equal token length, runs both through a forward pass, records activations h+ and h- at a chosen layer l, and computes the steering vector h_A = h+ - h-. This is rescaled by an injection coefficient c and added to the residual stream at layer l, position a=1 (front alignment), for a user prompt's forward pass, which then continues to generate steered output (Algorithm 1). No gradient descent or optimization is needed — it requires as few as 2 example prompts (versus ITI/Li et al. 2023b, which needs dozens of labeled samples and trained linear probes). Hyperparameters are the injection layer l (middle layers work best, |c|<15 typical) and coefficient c, tuned by simple grid search (c∈[3,20], l∈[6,24]). Tested primarily on GPT-2-XL (1.5B) for mechanistic/ablation experiments, and on OPT-6.7B and LLaMA-3-8B for toxicity (RealToxicityPrompts + Perspective API) and sentiment (Stanford IMDb + SiEBERT classifier) benchmarks, with replication checks on GPT-J-6B and LLaMA-1-13B.

Evaluations include: perplexity ratio on wedding-related vs unrelated OpenWebText sentences; token log-probability Q-Q plots; layer-sweep on topic-steering success rate; GPT-3.5-scored topic relevance across 6 topics; toxicity/fluency/relevance vs SOTA baselines (PREADD, FUDGE, Air-Decoding); sentiment steering success vs same baselines; and a ConceptNet/LAMA factual-knowledge P@K test to check for side effects on general capabilities. They also run controls (Appendix B/G) comparing ActAdd against literal token injection and random-vector injection to argue the effect isn't just "virtual prompting" or noise.

**Findings:** ActAdd achieves state-of-the-art negative-to-positive sentiment steering and beats prior toxicity-reduction methods (8% lower toxicity than best baseline on OPT, 5% drop on LLaMA-3) while incurring only modest fluency/relevance costs, and has negligible impact on ConceptNet factual-knowledge probabilities (i.e., general capabilities are preserved). Topic steering succeeds >90% of the time at the optimal GPT-2-XL injection layer (~layer 6) vs ~2% baseline, and effectiveness peaks at middle layers then declines. Steering vectors are far from negligible in magnitude — at effective coefficients the injected vector's norm can be ~10x the residual stream's own norm, implying the steering vector dominates over information from the actual prompt. Control experiments show ActAdd is not equivalent to prompting/token-injection (perplexity effects differ substantially) and is not just adding noise (random vectors of matching norm barely change outputs, unlike targeted steering vectors). Ablations show intervening on only a subset of residual-stream dimensions still partially works, suggesting a mix of distributed and axis-aligned feature representations. Limitations: requires access to intermediate activations (not usable via black-box APIs), free hyperparameters (l, c, alignment) reduce user-friendliness, and the method isn't expected to teach genuinely new skills the way finetuning can.

**Relevance:** This is a foundational activation-steering / linear-representation paper for the mech interp toolkit: it operationalizes and gives strong empirical support for the linear representation hypothesis (high-level semantic features like sentiment/topic as directions in residual-stream activation space that are causally manipulable and roughly invariant across contexts). For a multilingual/interlingua seminar, ActAdd is directly relevant methodologically — the same difference-of-activations technique (used here for Love-Hate, topic, toxicity) is exactly the kind of causal-intervention tool used in follow-up work to test whether multilingual models share language-neutral concept directions (e.g., extracting a "concept" vector from one language and transferring/steering with it in another). It's a key building block/precedent for interlingua-hypothesis experiments that inject or ablate shared representations across languages.

### Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits

**File:** `Olah-2020-Zoom-In-Introduction-to-Circuits.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Olah, Cammarata, Schubert, Goh, Petrov, Carter (OpenAI), 2020  
**Venue:** Distill (Circuits thread), DOI 10.23915/distill.00024.001  
**Type:** blog post/article (Distill journal, part of the "Circuits thread")

> Individual neurons in vision CNNs (studied via InceptionV1) are often understandable "features" corresponding to directions in activation space, and the weighted connections between them ("circuits") encode literal, hand-readable algorithms — a finding the authors argue could ground a "cellular biology of deep learning."

**Problem:** Most interpretability work seeks holistic, simplified explanations of an entire network's behavior, treating individual neurons/weights as too low-level to be worth serious study. The authors ask the inverse question: what happens if you "zoom in" — spend thousands of hours tracing individual neurons and their specific connections — the way microscopy/cellular biology or x-ray crystallography/molecular biology transformed their fields by changing the level of analysis, not just the precision.

**Method:** The paper is structured around three explicitly speculative, borrowed-from-cell-theory claims (echoing Schwann's 1839 cell theory claims): (1) Features — individual neurons (or directions in neuron activation space) are the fundamental, understandable unit of a network, corresponding to meaningful directions; (2) Circuits — features connect via literal weights into sub-graphs ("circuits") that implement legible algorithms, readable directly off the floating-point weights; (3) Universality — analogous features and circuits recur across different architectures and training runs. All examples are drawn from InceptionV1 trained on ImageNet. To validate that a neuron is a genuine "feature" rather than a projection of human expectation onto noise, they lay out a battery of seven converging evidentiary arguments (borrowed partly from visual neuroscience): (1) feature visualization (optimize input to maximize activation), (2) dataset examples (top-activating real images), (3) synthetic examples (rendered curves/shapes at controlled parameters), (4) joint tuning curves (response as stimulus is rotated), and three novel circuit-based arguments — (5) feature implementation (reading the causal algorithm off the incoming weights), (6) feature use (checking that downstream neurons use the feature as expected), and (7) handwritten circuits (hand-setting weights to reimplement the algorithm from scratch and confirming it reproduces the neuron's behavior).

**Findings:** Using these methods they walk through concrete circuits in InceptionV1: curve detectors in layer mixed3b (family of orientation-tuned units spanning 360°, responding to ~60px-radius curved boundaries) built from earlier curve/line detectors via a 5x5 conv whose weights literally trace a "tangent curve" template, with rotation of orientation producing rotation of the weight pattern ("equivariant circuit"); high-low frequency detectors in early layers (family units that detect high-frequency texture on one side of the receptive field and low-frequency on the other, useful as an out-of-focus-boundary heuristic); a pose-invariant dog head detector (neuron 4b:409) built via a 4-layer "unioning over cases" motif, where separate left-facing and right-facing pathways mutually inhibit each other at each stage before being unioned into an orientation-invariant unit (giving it XOR-like properties); and a car detector (mixed4c) assembled from window, car-body, and wheel detectors with position-dependent excitatory/inhibitory weights — but instead of a clean car neuron at the next layer, the car feature is spread ("superposition") across several dog-detector neurons, producing polysemantic neurons, since cars and dogs rarely co-occur so the model can pack more features into limited neurons. This yields the concept of "circuit motifs" (equivariance, unioning over cases, superposition) as recurring structural patterns worth studying in their own right, analogous to biological circuit motifs. For universality, they show (anecdotal, not rigorous) evidence that curve detectors and high-low frequency detectors recur across AlexNet, InceptionV1, VGG19, and ResNetV2-50, and across different training datasets (ImageNet vs Places365), suggesting convergent learning, though they explicitly caveat this evidence is not yet rigorous. They also flag polysemanticity as a major open obstacle to the circuits agenda.

**Relevance:** This is a foundational mechanistic interpretability paper establishing the features-as-directions and circuits-as-legible-algorithms framework, plus the concept of superposition (later central to toy-models-of-superposition and sparse autoencoder work) and universality (convergent feature formation across models) — both directly relevant to the interlingua hypothesis in multilingual LMs, which is essentially a universality/shared-representation claim at the level of language rather than vision architecture. The methodological toolkit here (activation maximization, dataset examples, weight-based circuit tracing, ablation-style "handwritten circuit" reconstruction) is the direct ancestor of circuit-tracing approaches now applied to language models to test whether multilingual models route through shared, language-neutral intermediate representations.

### Interpretability in the Wild: a Circuit for Indirect Object Identification in GPT-2 small

**File:** `Wang-2023-Interpretability-in-the-Wild-IOI-Circuit.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Wang, Variengien, Conmy, Shlegeris & Steinhardt (Redwood Research / UC Berkeley), 2022 (arXiv Nov 2022)  
**Type:** paper

> The paper reverse-engineers, in full end-to-end detail, the 26-attention-head circuit GPT-2 small uses to solve indirect object identification (IOI), and proposes faithfulness/completeness/minimality as formal criteria for judging whether a proposed circuit really explains a model behavior.

**Problem:** Mechanistic interpretability work up to this point had either explained toy behaviors in tiny models or gestured broadly at what large models do, without a rigorous, fully-worked example of reverse-engineering a real natural-language behavior "in the wild." The authors want to show that a detailed circuit-level explanation of a nontrivial linguistic task is achievable in a real (if small) language model, and to formalize what it even means for a circuit explanation to be "correct."

**Method:** They study GPT-2 small (12 layers, 12 heads/layer) on the IOI task: sentences like "When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to ___" should complete with the indirect object "Mary," not the repeated subject "John." They build a templated dataset (15 templates, random single-token names/places/items) and use two metrics: logit difference (IO logit minus S logit; baseline 3.56, IO predicted 99.3% of the time) and IO probability (baseline 49%). The core method is "path patching," a causal-intervention technique generalizing causal mediation analysis (Vig et al. 2020): it isolates the *direct* effect of one attention head on a downstream component by patching only the paths from that head to the target (holding other paths fixed), using mean-ablation over a reference distribution p_ABC (three unrelated random names) rather than zero-ablation, since zero is an arbitrary/noisy baseline. Starting from the logits and iteratively tracing backward (queries, then keys/values of upstream heads), they combine path patching with attention-pattern analysis, OV-matrix "copy score" probes, and knockout/ablation experiments, plus checks on synthetic repeated-token sequences to validate induction/previous-token behavior independent of IOI.

**Findings:** They identify a 26-head circuit (1.1% of head×position pairs) organized into 7 head classes implementing a 3-step algorithm (find prior names → remove duplicates → output the remainder): Duplicate Token Heads (detect repeated names), Previous Token Heads + Induction Heads (redundantly signal duplication/position via an induction mechanism), S-Inhibition Heads (write position+token "signals" into Name Mover queries to suppress attention to the subject), Name Mover Heads (attend to and copy the IO token into the output; copy score >95%), Negative Name Mover Heads (write in the *opposite* direction, hedging predictions), and Backup Name Mover Heads (dormant heads that take over Name Mover function only when the primary ones are ablated — evidence of learned redundancy, possibly from dropout). They formalize three validation criteria: faithfulness (circuit recovers 87% of full-model logit difference), completeness (circuit behaves like the full model even under further knockouts — mostly holds except for greedy-adversarial subsets that recover up to 87% of missing performance, revealing residual, hard-to-interpret redundancy), and minimality (every head has nontrivial, ≥1%, individual contribution). They also build adversarial examples exploiting the discovered duplicate-detection mechanism (duplicating the IO name), which flips the model's prediction toward S 23% of the time.

**Relevance:** This is a canonical circuits/mechanistic-interpretability methods paper (path patching, ablation criteria) rather than a multilingual/interlingua paper — it does not address cross-lingual representations. Its relevance to the seminar is primarily methodological: it is a template for how one would rigorously identify and validate a circuit (e.g., a hypothesized shared "interlingua" circuit) using causal interventions and the faithfulness/completeness/minimality framework, and its finding of redundant/backup heads and negative-writing heads is a cautionary example of how naive circuit claims can be misleading — directly useful for evaluating claims about shared multilingual circuits.

### Feature Visualization: How neural networks build up their understanding of images

**File:** `Olah-2017-Feature-Visualization.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Olah, Mordvintsev & Schubert, 2017  
**Type:** blog post/article (Distill journal, treated as a peer-reviewed paper)

> Optimizing input images by gradient ascent to maximally activate a chosen neuron/channel/layer/class logit reveals what that unit is "looking for," but doing this naively produces adversarial noise, so the paper's real contribution is a set of regularization/parameterization tricks (transformation robustness, decorrelated Fourier-space preconditioning, diversity terms) that make the resulting visualizations interpretable.

**Problem:** How can we generate images that reveal what individual units (neurons, channels, layers) or output classes inside a trained CNN have learned to detect, in a way that is faithful to what actually drives the network's behavior (as opposed to merely correlating with it, which is a risk of picking dataset examples)?

**Method:** The core technique is gradient-based optimization: start from random noise and iteratively update pixel values via backprop to maximize an activation objective, exploiting the fact that CNNs are differentiable w.r.t. their input. The paper defines a taxonomy of objectives — single neuron (layer[x,y,z]), channel (layer[:,:,z], used for most figures), whole layer/DeepDream (layer[:,:,:]), class logits pre-softmax, and class probabilities post-softmax (they find pre-softmax logits give better visual quality). Applied to GoogLeNet trained on ImageNet, they show a progression from edges (conv2d0) to textures (mixed3a) to patterns (mixed4a) to parts (mixed4b/c) to objects (mixed4d/e). To combat the central failure mode — unconstrained optimization converging on high-frequency "adversarial" noise patterns caused partly by strided convolutions/pooling — they survey a spectrum of regularization strategies: frequency penalization (total variation, blurring, bilateral filters), transformation robustness (stochastic jitter/rotation/scaling applied before each optimization step), and learned priors (GAN/VAE latent-space optimization, or joint optimization with a learned data-density prior, producing near-photorealistic but prior-contaminated images). Their own preferred recipe (detailed in a footnote) combines transformation robustness with "preconditioning": performing gradient descent not in raw pixel space but in a decorrelated, whitened Fourier basis (with per-frequency energy scaling and Cholesky-decorrelated color channels), run for 2560 Adam steps at lr=0.05. They also introduce a cosine-similarity-based "diversity" term added to the objective to force multiple simultaneous optimizations to diverge, exposing different facets of what a neuron responds to, and use joint/interpolated optimization of neuron pairs to probe how features interact in "activation space" (treating neuron activations as basis vectors and asking whether random directions in this space are similarly interpretable — building on Szegedy et al. 2013 and Bau/Zhou et al. 2017's Network Dissection).

**Findings:** Diversity-regularized optimization shows single neurons often respond to multiple distinct "facets" (e.g., a neuron that fires on soccer balls, golf balls, and tennis balls, or one that mixes cat/fox faces with car bodies) — evidence that individual neurons are not always clean, unitary semantic units. Random directions in activation space are somewhat interpretable but less reliably so than basis (single-neuron) directions, reconciling earlier conflicting claims. The decorrelated Fourier-space preconditioner is framed as the paper's biggest technical contribution: it doesn't change the optimization's minima but reshapes the descent geometry, dramatically suppressing high-frequency noise and producing better, faster-converging visualizations than raw-pixel or blurring-based approaches. Combining preconditioning with transformation robustness (jitter, rotation, scaling, padding) yields their best-quality results.

**Relevance:** This is foundational mechanistic-interpretability methodology (from the Circuits/Distill lineage that predates and informs current MI work) for probing what individual units/directions in a network represent — directly relevant to a seminar on the interlingua hypothesis, since analogous "what does this unit detect" questions in multilingual LMs (e.g., is a given neuron/direction language-specific or language-neutral) require comparable activation-maximization or probing techniques, and the paper's caution that neurons/directions are not always clean semantic units is a key methodological warning for anyone using feature visualization or directions to argue for shared cross-lingual representations.

### A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits

**File:** `Elhage-2021-Mathematical-Framework-for-Transformer-Circuits.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Elhage, Nanda, Olsson, et al. (Anthropic), 2021  
**Type:** blog post/article (Transformer Circuits Thread — long-form technical research article)

> By rewriting attention-only transformers as a sum of linear "end-to-end path" terms (via the residual stream, OV, and QK circuits) rather than as sequential layers, the authors mechanistically reverse-engineer toy 0/1/2-layer models and discover "induction heads" — a general in-context-learning mechanism built from head composition.

**Problem:** Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer the actual computations transformers perform (analogous to decompiling a binary into source code), so that safety-relevant model behaviors can be understood systematically rather than discovered by accident. Prior circuits work (Distill's InceptionV1 thread) succeeded for vision CNNs, but no comparable systematic account existed for transformer language models, and attention layers pose new challenges (no privileged basis in the residual stream, bilinear rather than purely linear operations).

**Method:** The authors study maximally simplified "toy" models: attention-only transformers (no MLPs, no biases, no layernorm) with 0, 1, or 2 layers. Key mathematical moves: (1) treat attention heads as independent, additive functions writing into a shared linear "residual stream," so any pair of layers has implicit "virtual weights" (products of one layer's output weights and another's input weights); (2) use the "path expansion trick" — expand the product-of-layers computation into a sum over end-to-end paths from input tokens to output logits, each path linear once attention patterns are frozen; (3) decompose each attention head into two independent low-rank matrices — the QK ("query-key") circuit governing the attention pattern (which token to move info from/to) and the OV ("output-value") circuit governing what information is written to the logits if attended to; (4) for two-layer models, formalize three kinds of head composition (Q-, K-, V-composition, i.e., which projection of a later head reads a subspace written by an earlier head) and use ablation (via an algorithm freezing attention patterns and truncating path order) plus eigenvalue analysis (sum of positive eigenvalues relative to total, as a "copying" detector) to empirically test mechanistic hypotheses.

**Findings:** Zero-layer transformers just learn bigram statistics (accessible directly from W_U W_E). One-layer attention-only transformers behave as an ensemble of bigram and "skip-trigram" (A…BC) lookup tables read directly off the expanded OV/QK matrices; most heads devote large capacity to token "copying" (detected via positive-eigenvalue-dominant OV matrices, e.g. 10/12 heads in one model), which the authors characterize as a primitive, factored (and sometimes literally buggy, e.g. "keep…in bay") form of in-context learning. Two-layer attention-only models gain a qualitatively more powerful mechanism: "induction heads," second-layer heads that via K-composition with a first-layer previous-token head search the context for a prior occurrence of the current token and copy whatever followed it ([a][b]…[a]→[b]), verified to work even on random token sequences and shown (via ablation) to be the dominant source of the performance gain from depth, while pure V-composition ("virtual heads") turned out to matter little in these small models. The paper explicitly promises (and a later companion paper delivers) evidence that induction heads persist and drive in-context learning in much larger, realistic language models — though MLP layers (2/3+ of parameters) remain largely uninterpreted, a stated major limitation.

**Relevance:** This is foundational infrastructure for mechanistic interpretability generally, not directly about multilingual models or the interlingua hypothesis — it introduces the residual-stream-as-communication-channel view, QK/OV circuit decomposition, and the discovery of induction heads that underlie most subsequent circuits/features work. For an interlingua-hypothesis seminar, its main relevance is methodological: the same "linear paths through the residual stream" and head-composition machinery is exactly what later work uses to ask whether multilingual models route different languages through shared subspaces/circuits (e.g., whether induction-head-like or "copying" mechanisms and shared OV/QK structure are language-agnostic), so students should read this as the toolkit prerequisite before tackling papers that examine shared vs. language-specific circuits/representations across languages.

### In-context Learning and Induction Heads

**File:** `Olsson-2022-InContext-Learning-and-Induction-Heads.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Olsson, Elhage, Nanda, Joseph, DasSarma, Henighan, Mann, et al. (Anthropic), 2022  
**Type:** paper

> Presents six lines of indirect/circumstantial evidence that "induction heads" — a two-head attention circuit that copies/completes repeated token patterns ([A][B]...[A]→[B]) — are the primary mechanism driving in-context learning in transformer language models of all sizes, from tiny 2-layer attention-only models up to a 13B-parameter, 40-layer model.

**Problem:** Follow-up to "A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits," which fully reverse-engineered induction heads only in tiny 1-2 layer attention-only toy models. The open question: does the same induction-head mechanism actually explain in-context learning (ICL) in real, large, MLP-containing transformers, where direct mathematical reverse-engineering is intractable? The paper asks whether mechanistic findings from toy models generalize to frontier-scale models via indirect/empirical evidence rather than full circuit analysis.

**Method:** Defines ICL operationally as an "in-context learning score" = loss at the 500th token minus loss at the 50th token in a 512-token context (a macro, Kaplan-et-al.-style definition, distinct from Brown et al.'s few-shot framing). Defines induction heads empirically via two measurable properties on repeated random-token sequences: "prefix matching" (attends to the token that followed the current token's previous occurrence) and "copying" (raises the logit of the attended token). Analyzes 34 decoder-only transformers (small attention-only 1-6 layer, small with MLPs, full-scale models up to 40 layers/13B params, and a custom "smeared key" architecture) across training snapshots, using per-token loss PCA, attention-head ablations (>50,000), and a "smeared key" architectural intervention that lets 1-layer models express induction-head-like behavior. Six arguments: (1) macroscopic co-occurrence of a training "phase change" (~2.5-5B tokens) where induction heads and ICL both form abruptly; (2) co-perturbation via the smeared-key architecture shifting when/whether the phase change occurs; (3) direct ablation of induction heads in small models tanking ICL; (4) qualitative examples of induction heads in the 13B model performing literal copying, translation (EN/FR/DE), and abstract pattern-matching/function-like behavior; (5) mechanistic reverse-engineering (QK/OV circuit analysis, K-composition with "previous-token heads") showing why induction heads should causally contribute to ICL; (6) continuity of phase-change phenomena from small to large models as an extrapolation argument.

**Findings:** Evidence strength varies sharply by claim and model size: for small attention-only models, ablations give strong causal evidence induction heads drive the *majority* of ICL; for models with MLPs and full-scale models, evidence is only medium/correlational for the "majority" claim (confounds include possible co-forming alternative composition mechanisms). The phase change is a real, robust phenomenon — a visible non-convex "bump" in the training loss curve, present in >1-layer models but absent in 1-layer models (which also lack induction heads and show little ICL). The smeared-key intervention causes 1-layer models to gain a phase change and ICL where vanilla 1-layer models have none, and makes the phase change occur earlier in deeper models — supporting causality. In the 13B model, single attention heads simultaneously satisfy the strict induction-head definition AND perform translation and abstract pattern-matching, suggesting the same circuit generalizes to "fuzzy nearest-neighbor" completion ([A*][B*]...[A]→[B]). A curious unexplained finding: post-phase-change ICL score is roughly constant (~0.4 nats) regardless of model size, because larger models extract most of their relative advantage very early in the context rather than showing more "ICL" by this metric. The authors are explicit the case is circumstantial, not proven, and list confounds throughout.

**Relevance:** Directly relevant to mechanistic interpretability methodology (circuit-level claims, ablations, QK/OV decomposition) and indirectly relevant to multilingual/interlingua discussions: Argument 4 documents a single attention head (layer 7 of the 40-layer model) that performs induction-head-style translation between English, French, and German, attending to the token about to be translated in a manner structurally identical to literal copying but operating on cross-lingual "similar" tokens (A*≈A, B*≈B) rather than identical ones. This is an early, concrete example of a shared/language-general circuit mechanism operating across languages inside a single model — a mechanistic building block relevant to arguments about shared multilingual representations, though the paper itself does not address the interlingua hypothesis directly and treats translation as one of several anecdotal "abstract induction" examples rather than a systematic multilingual study.

## Week 6 Thu (Feb 12) / Week 7 Thu (Feb 19) — Cross-lingual Representations

### How multilingual is Multilingual BERT?

**File:** `Pires-2019-How-Multilingual-is-Multilingual-BERT.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Pires, Schlinger & Garrette, 2019  
**Venue:** ACL 2019 (Proceedings of the 57th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics), pp. 4996-5001  
**Type:** paper

> Through a series of zero-shot cross-lingual transfer probes, the paper shows that Multilingual BERT (M-BERT) — trained only on concatenated monolingual Wikipedia corpora in 104 languages with no cross-lingual signal — nonetheless learns a shared, language-neutral representation space, but one with systematic gaps for typologically distant languages and transliterated scripts.

**Problem:** Contextualized language models like BERT had mostly been probed for what they learn about a single language (English). This paper asks whether a single model pretrained jointly (but monolingually, language-by-language) on many languages' Wikipedia text, with a shared WordPiece vocabulary and no explicit alignment objective (no parallel data, no language ID token, no translation loss), ends up building genuinely shared multilingual representations — i.e., does an "interlingua" emerge for free from massively multilingual pretraining alone?

**Method:** The authors use M-BERT's zero-shot cross-lingual transfer setup as an experimental probe: fine-tune the 12-layer transformer on a downstream task in one language, evaluate in another, with no task data in the target language. Tasks: NER (CoNLL-2002/2003 for Dutch/Spanish/English/German, plus an in-house 16-language NER set) and POS tagging (Universal Dependencies, 41 languages). Key probes: (1) vocabulary-overlap analysis — correlating NER F1 with WordPiece overlap between train/eval entity vocabularies (overlap = |E_train∩E_eval|/|E_train∪E_eval|), comparing M-BERT to EN-BERT (English-only BERT); (2) cross-script transfer (e.g., Urdu→Hindi, different scripts); (3) typological-similarity analysis using WALS features (word order: SVO/SOV, adjective-noun order) correlated with transfer accuracy; (4) code-switching/transliteration tests on a Hindi-English UD corpus (Bhat et al. 2018); (5) a representation-geometry experiment: sampling 5000 sentence pairs from WMT16, extracting mean-pooled per-layer hidden states, computing an average per-layer translation vector between language pairs (e.g., v̄_EN→DE), and measuring "nearest-neighbor translation accuracy" — whether translating an English sentence vector by this single fixed offset lands nearest to the correct German sentence vector, layer by layer.

**Findings:** M-BERT achieves substantial zero-shot transfer (e.g., NER F1 ~70-80 for EN→DE/NL/ES; POS accuracy >80% across European language pairs). Critically, unlike EN-BERT (whose transfer performance tracks WordPiece overlap almost linearly and collapses near zero for different-script pairs), M-BERT's performance is largely flat/independent of lexical overlap (Figure 1) — e.g., Urdu-finetuned M-BERT gets 91% POS accuracy on Hindi despite zero shared script/vocabulary — showing transfer goes deeper than vocabulary memorization. Transfer correlates positively with typological similarity (shared WALS word-order features; Figure 2, Table 5): SVO↔SVO and matching adjective-noun order pairs transfer much better than mismatched ones (e.g., SVO-trained→SOV eval POS drops to ~66.5% vs 81.6% within SVO), implying M-BERT maps vocabulary into a shared space but doesn't learn systematic syntactic reordering transformations. Monolingual pretraining alone lets M-BERT generalize well to code-switched text (comparable to specialized code-switching systems) but it fails on transliterated text it never saw in pretraining (accuracy drops from 86.6% to 50.4% on transliterated vs. script-corrected Hindi), indicating language-specific pretraining exposure, not just shared subword vocabulary, drives transfer. The representation-geometry experiment shows a single average per-layer "translation vector" between languages achieves >50% nearest-neighbor accuracy at matching translated sentences across most layers (peaking mid-network, degrading in top layers, which the authors attribute to language-specific information needed for the LM objective), directly evidencing a shared, largely language-agnostic linear subspace. Authors hypothesize shared cross-lingual anchors (numbers, URLs, proper nouns appearing in all languages) get pulled into a common space during joint WordPiece training, dragging co-occurring pieces along with them.

**Relevance:** This is a foundational empirical anchor for the interlingua hypothesis: it's the paper that first showed, via zero-shot transfer and the WMT16 sentence-vector nearest-neighbor experiment, that a jointly-pretrained multilingual transformer with no explicit alignment objective spontaneously organizes a shared cross-lingual representational subspace in its middle layers. It's directly useful for mech interp discussions because it (a) operationalizes "shared representation" via a simple linear offset/nearest-neighbor geometric test rather than just downstream task metrics, (b) localizes where the shared subspace is strongest (middle layers) vs. where language-specific info dominates (bottom layers = surface/token info, top layers = needed for the LM prediction objective), and (c) documents concrete failure modes (typological mismatch, transliteration) that any later mechanistic account of multilingual representations needs to explain.

### Beto, Bentz, Becas: The Surprising Cross-Lingual Effectiveness of BERT

**File:** `Wu-Dredze-2019-Beto-Bentz-Becas.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Shijie Wu and Mark Dredze, 2019  
**Type:** paper

> Multilingual BERT (mBERT), trained only on concatenated single-language Wikipedia text with no explicit cross-lingual signal (no parallel data, no bilingual dictionaries, no shared objective across languages), nonetheless produces representations that support strong zero-shot cross-lingual transfer across 39 languages and 5 diverse NLP tasks, competitive with or beating methods that use explicit cross-lingual supervision.

**Problem:** Does jointly pretraining a single contextual model (BERT) across 104 languages, with the only cross-lingual "glue" being a shared WordPiece subword vocabulary and no explicit alignment signal (no parallel sentences, no bilingual dictionaries, no shared cross-lingual objective), actually produce a shared/language-neutral representation space that supports zero-shot cross-lingual transfer? The paper interrogates whether mBERT's strong performance on XNLI (shown by prior work) generalizes to a broad range of task types, and probes the mechanism behind it.

**Method:** The authors fine-tune mBERT (12 layers, 12 attention heads, d_h=768, 179M params, ~120k WordPiece vocab, base cased multilingual checkpoint) in a zero-shot cross-lingual transfer setup: fine-tune on English-labeled training data only, then evaluate directly on target-language test data with no target-language supervision. Five tasks/datasets: (1) document classification on MLDoc (Reuters, 8 languages, 4-way topic classification); (2) natural language inference on XNLI (15 languages, 3-way entailment classification); (3) NER on CoNLL 2002/2003 + a Chinese NER dataset (4+1 languages, BIO tagging, F1); (4) POS tagging on a Universal Dependencies v1.4 subset (15 languages, accuracy), following Kim et al. 2017's setup; (5) dependency parsing on UD v2.2 (31 languages, UAS/LAS), using Dozat & Manning's (2016) graph-based biaffine parser with mBERT replacing the LSTM encoder, following Ahmad et al. (2019). Task-specific heads are linear/softmax classifiers on top of mBERT's final layer (or first-subword representations for word-level tasks). Beyond the main transfer experiments, they run four targeted probes: (Q1) benchmark mBERT against best published cross-lingual-embedding and bitext-supervised baselines per task; (Q2) compare fine-tuning-all-parameters vs. a feature-based approach (frozen mBERT + trained BiLSTM on a learned weighted combination of all 13 layers, ELMo-style) vs. freezing the bottom n∈{0,3,6,9} layers during fine-tuning, to see how representations vary by depth; (Q3) test whether mBERT retains language-specific information despite cross-lingual abstraction, via a language-ID probe (WiLI-2018 dataset, 99 languages) using frozen bag-of-words mBERT representations from each layer with a simple linear classifier; (Q4) quantify subword sharing across languages (type-level and token-level overlap between English training vocab and target-language test vocab) and correlate it (Pearson r) with zero-shot transfer performance per task.

Optimization details: Adam (β1=0.9, β2=0.999), L2 weight decay 0.01, warmup over first 10% of batches then linear decay, max subword sequence length 128 (with a 64-subword sliding-window for NER/POS), hyperparameter search over learning rate {2e-5, 3e-5, 5e-5}, batch size {16,32}, epochs {3,4}, selected via English dev performance only (no target-language dev data used).

**Findings:** mBERT is a strong zero-shot cross-lingual transfer learner across all five tasks, generally competitive with or exceeding cross-lingual word-embedding baselines and even some models trained with explicit cross-lingual bitext/dictionary supervision, despite having none itself: it beats MultiCCA and is close to a bitext-pretrained sentence model on MLDoc; beats an unsupervised bilingual-embedding NER baseline by 6.9 F1 on average (11.8 on German); beats Kim et al.'s (2017) POS model that uses a small amount of target-language supervision (even outperforming it in German/Polish/Slovak/Swedish); and beats Ahmad et al.'s (2019) dependency parsing baseline by 7.3 UAS / 0.4 LAS on average (gold POS tags help further, +1.6 UAS/+4.7 LAS). On XNLI it underperforms models with explicit cross-lingual signal (e.g., Lample & Conneau's MLM+TLM) but beats a comparable model trained with only English+15-language MLM and no bitext, and outperforms distantly-related-language transfer (Chinese, Russian) more than closely related Indo-European transfer relative to the Artetxe & Schwenk (2018) baseline.

Layer-wise analysis (Q2): freezing the bottom n layers during fine-tuning improves performance over full fine-tuning in all five tasks — sentence-level tasks (classification, NLI) benefit most from freezing more layers (n=6), word-level tasks (NER, POS, parsing) benefit most at n=3; a frozen feature-based approach with a trained BiLSTM on top consistently underperforms fine-tuning.

Language-ID probe (Q3): mBERT retains substantial language-specific information at every layer (~96% language-ID accuracy from bag-of-words representations across all 13 layers, with no clear degradation at higher layers), meaning mBERT does not fully abstract away language identity even as it supports cross-lingual transfer — the two properties coexist.

Subword-sharing analysis (Q4): the degree of WordPiece vocabulary overlap between the English training set and a target language's test set (measured at both type-level and token-level) correlates positively (Pearson r>0.5, p<0.05) with zero-shot transfer performance in 4 of 5 tasks (not XNLI), and shared subwords tend to be high-frequency ones — suggesting lexical/subword overlap is a real (if partial) mechanism behind mBERT's cross-lingual transfer, and could serve as a heuristic for choosing a source language.

**Relevance:** This is a foundational empirical paper directly bearing on the interlingua hypothesis for multilingual models: it shows that shared-subword, jointly-pretrained masked-LM training without any explicit cross-lingual alignment objective produces representations that generalize across languages well enough for zero-shot transfer on syntax (POS, parsing), semantics/entailment (NLI), and classification/tagging tasks — but crucially also shows the representation is NOT a "pure" language-neutral interlingua: language identity remains linearly decodable at ~96% accuracy from every layer, and transfer quality correlates with surface-level subword overlap rather than being uniformly high across all language pairs. This complicates a naive interlingua reading (fully shared, language-agnostic space) and instead supports a picture where mBERT learns a partially-shared space intertwined with retained language-specific and lexical-overlap-driven structure — a key empirical anchor for later mechanistic work probing whether/where language-neutral subspaces exist inside multilingual transformers.

### On the Cross-lingual Transferability of Monolingual Representations

**File:** `Wang-2020-Crosslingual-Transferability-of-Monolingual-Reps.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Artetxe, Ruder, and Yogatama, 2020  
**Type:** paper

> A monolingual BERT can be transferred to new languages by only relearning the embedding layer (keeping the transformer body frozen), and this performs competitively with jointly-trained multilingual models like mBERT — implying shared vocabulary and joint multilingual pretraining are not what makes cross-lingual generalization work.

**Problem:** The paper interrogates the prevailing explanation (from Pires et al. 2019, echoed by Cao et al. 2020 and Wu & Dredze 2019) for why mBERT-style models generalize zero-shot across languages: the hypothesis that (i) shared subword vocabulary items act as "anchor points," (ii) joint training across languages spreads this alignment, and (iii) this jointly yields deep cross-lingual representations. The authors ask whether these three ingredients are actually necessary.

**Method:** They design MONOTRANS, a procedure that deliberately violates all three assumed ingredients. Four steps: (1) pretrain a monolingual transformer (BERT-base architecture) in English with MLM+NSP; (2) transfer it to a new language L2 by freezing the entire transformer body and learning only a new token-embedding matrix from scratch via MLM+NSP on an L2 monolingual (Wikipedia) corpus, using a separate SentencePiece vocabulary per language (no shared subwords); (3) fine-tune on a downstream task using English labeled data while keeping embeddings frozen; (4) zero-shot transfer to L2 by swapping in the L2 embeddings learned in step 2. Extensions tested: language-specific position embeddings, Gaussian-noised fine-tuning (to make the frozen body robust to the embedding-layer train/test mismatch), and residual adapter modules (Houlsby et al. 2019) inserted into each transformer layer during L2 transfer. They compare against JOINTMULTI (mBERT-like, 15 languages jointly, vocab sizes 32k-200k), JOINTPAIR (bilingual joint models, joint vs. disjoint vocab, to control for language count), and CLWE (skip-gram embeddings mapped via VecMap, then BERT trained on top of frozen mapped embeddings). Evaluated on XNLI, MLDoc, and PAWS-X (zero-shot transfer from English), plus a newly released benchmark, XQuAD (240 SQuAD v1.1 paragraphs, 1190 QA pairs, professionally translated into 10 languages) for extractive QA, which requires span-level structural transfer rather than sentence classification. They also run semantic (WiC, SCWS) and syntactic (Marvin & Linzen 2018 targeted syntactic evaluation) probing on non-English-to-English transferred representations.

**Findings:** MONOTRANS is competitive with joint multilingual training despite having no shared vocabulary and no joint pretraining: on XNLI the gap to the best JOINTPAIR model shrinks from 3.3 points (basic) to 1.1 points with position embeddings + noised fine-tuning; on PAWS-X the gap is only 0.6 points; on MLDoc results are comparable across all model families. On the harder XQuAD task the gap is larger, but adding adapters closes it, making MONOTRANS comparable to the best joint system. A shared subword vocabulary turns out not to be necessary at all for JOINTMULTI either — JOINTPAIR with disjoint per-language vocabularies actually outperforms joint-vocabulary variants, and the key factor is "effective vocabulary size per language" (larger vocab helps, more languages competing for a shared vocab hurts). CLWE (classic embedding-space mapping) is competitive only on the easiest/smallest task (MLDoc) and performs poorly on XNLI/XQuAD, suggesting fixed linear mappings between embedding spaces are too restrictive compared to MONOTRANS's frozen-body approach. Probing results: monolingual semantic representations transfer well across languages (WiC, SCWS scores close to English-trained models), but syntactic abstractions transfer less well (larger gaps in the Marvin & Linzen syntactic evaluation), suggesting semantic abstractions generalize more readily than syntactic ones. Overall conclusion: deep monolingual transformer bodies already learn some language-general/universal abstractions that a lexical-level (embedding-only) alignment can tap into — contradicting the "anchor points + joint training" explanation for mBERT's cross-lingual ability.

**Relevance:** Directly relevant to the interlingua hypothesis: this paper is essentially a controlled ablation attacking the naive "shared subwords + joint training" account of multilingual representation sharing, and provides evidence that language-general/interlingua-like abstractions can emerge in a purely monolingual transformer body — i.e., "interlinguality" may be a property of deep transformer representations generally (perhaps induced by the MLM objective and architecture) rather than something requiring explicit multilingual joint training. This reframes what mechanistic interpretability work on multilingual models should look for: the locus of cross-lingual sharing may be less about vocabulary-level anchoring and more about abstractions in the frozen transformer layers themselves, which is a testable mechanistic claim (e.g., probing/circuit analysis of which layers carry the transferable abstractions).

### Cross-Lingual Ability of Multilingual BERT: An Empirical Study

**File:** `Karthikeyan-2020-Crosslingual-Ability-of-Multilingual-BERT.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Karthikeyan K, Zihan Wang, Stephen Mayhew, Dan Roth (2020)  
**Type:** paper

> Systematically ablates B-BERT (bilingual BERT) along linguistic, architectural, and objective dimensions to show that shared vocabulary is largely irrelevant to cross-lingual transfer, while structural language similarity and network depth are the real drivers.

**Problem:** Multilingual BERT (M-BERT) transfers surprisingly well across 104 languages despite no cross-lingual training signal (no parallel data, no alignment objective). Prior work (Pires et al. 2019; Wu & Dredze 2019) had hypothesized this arises mainly from shared word-pieces (lexical overlap) between languages, but treated M-BERT as a black box. This paper asks: which specific components — linguistic properties of the languages, model architecture, or training objective/input representation — actually cause cross-lingual generalization?

**Method:** To isolate variables, the authors study B-BERT, a bilingual (2-language) version of BERT trained from scratch on a source-target pair, avoiding interference from a third language. Their key methodological trick is constructing "Fake-English" (enfake): they shift the Unicode codepoint of every character in English Wikipedia text by a constant offset, producing a corpus identical to English in every structural/statistical property but sharing zero word-pieces with any real language. This lets them cleanly separate "lexical/word-piece overlap" from "structural similarity" (morphology, word order, word frequency distribution) as competing explanations. They pretrain B-BERT on English–Spanish, English–Russian, and English–Hindi pairs (chosen for varying script and typology) and evaluate on two downstream tasks: cross-lingual XNLI (textual entailment, train on English, test on target language, report accuracy) and cross-lingual NER on the LORELEI dataset (via a Bi-LSTM-CRF probe on frozen BERT features, F1 score). They then run controlled architecture ablations (depth, number of attention heads, total parameter count, holding others fixed) and input/objective ablations (next-sentence-prediction on/off, language-ID markers in input, character vs. word-piece vs. word tokenization), plus a word-order permutation experiment (randomly swapping 25/50/100% of word-piece pairs during pretraining) and a word-frequency-only corpus (sentences generated by sampling words from unigram frequency alone) to probe what "structural similarity" consists of.

**Findings:** (1) Word-piece/lexical overlap contributes almost nothing: B-BERT trained on Fake-English (zero shared vocabulary) transfers to Spanish/Hindi/Russian nearly as well as real English does (e.g., XNLI accuracy drops only ~0.5-1.4 points when overlap is removed), directly contradicting the Pires et al. hypothesis. (2) Structural similarity (word order, morphology-adjacent statistics) matters much more: destroying word order via permutation during pretraining causes large performance drops (e.g., NER F1 on Hindi falls from 62.9 to 2.9 at 100% permutation), yet performance remains well above random, implying other structural cues beyond word order also contribute; unigram word-frequency alone is insufficient for transfer (XNLI collapses to ~34-36%, near chance). (3) Architecture: network depth and total parameter count are crucial for both monolingual and cross-lingual performance (deeper models show smaller Fake-English-vs-target accuracy gaps), but the number of attention heads barely matters — a single-head B-BERT performs comparably to a 12-head one. (4) Objective/input: the next-sentence-prediction (NSP) objective hurts cross-lingual performance (removing it improves both XNLI and NER); adding explicit language-identity markers to the input has no effect on cross-lingual ability; word-piece and word-level tokenization both outperform character-level tokenization. (5) A side finding: entailment accuracy drops sharply when premise and hypothesis are in different languages, suggesting BERT may rely partly on surface word/phrase matching for NLI rather than pure semantic representation.

**Relevance:** Directly bears on the interlingua hypothesis by dismantling one of its leading mechanistic explanations (shared subword vocabulary as the anchor forcing a joint embedding space) and replacing it with "structural similarity" and model depth as the operative factors — though the paper explicitly leaves "structural similarity" underspecified/undissected, which is a natural opening for mechanistic interpretability work (e.g., probing what depth-dependent circuits or representations actually encode this structure, or what "language-neutral" subspaces look like given word-piece overlap is not the cause). The Fake-English methodology is also a reusable causal-intervention technique for interpretability researchers studying multilingual representations.

### XTREME: A Massively Multilingual Multi-task Benchmark for Evaluating Cross-lingual Generalization

**File:** `Hu-2020-XTREME-Benchmark.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Hu, Ruder, Siddhant, Neubig, Firat, Johnson, 2020  
**Type:** paper

> Introduces a 40-language, 9-task benchmark for zero-shot cross-lingual transfer and shows that even state-of-the-art multilingual encoders (mBERT, XLM, XLM-R, MMTE) fall well short of English/human performance, especially on syntactic and sentence-retrieval tasks and for non-Indo-European languages.

**Problem:** Prior evaluation of multilingual representations was fragmented — restricted to a handful of tasks (mostly translation/classification) and typologically similar languages — making it impossible to assess whether models are truly general-purpose cross-lingual encoders. No benchmark existed with broad task and language coverage under a consistent zero-shot transfer protocol.

**Method:** XTREME assembles 9 tasks across 4 categories: (1) sentence-pair classification — XNLI (entailment, 15 langs) and PAWS-X (paraphrase, 7 langs); (2) structured prediction — POS tagging (Universal Dependencies v2.5, 33/90 langs, F1 over 17 universal tags) and NER (WikiAnn, 40/176 langs, F1); (3) question answering — XQuAD, MLQA, and TyDiQA-GoldP (span extraction, F1/EM); (4) sentence retrieval — BUCC and Tatoeba (cosine-similarity nearest-neighbor retrieval, F1/accuracy). Covers 40 typologically diverse languages across 12 families selected via Wikipedia size and family coverage criteria. The protocol is strict zero-shot transfer: fine-tune on English-only labeled data, evaluate on target-language test sets, no target-language training data used for the main setting. Pseudo (machine-translated) test sets extend XNLI/XQuAD analysis to all 40 languages. Baselines compared: mBERT, XLM, XLM-R Large, MMTE (translation encoder), plus translate-train, translate-train multi-task, translate-test, in-language (full/1000-shot/multi-task) models, and human performance estimates.

**Findings:** XLM-R Large is the best zero-shot model (avg 68.2) but still trails human/English performance substantially, with the largest cross-lingual transfer gaps on QA (XQuAD, MLQA) and especially structured prediction (POS, NER) and sentence retrieval (BUCC, Tatoeba), where larger pretraining data barely helps versus classification tasks. Performance correlates strongly with pretraining (Wikipedia) data size for classification/QA (Pearson ρ≈0.8) but weakly for structured prediction (ρ≈0.35), suggesting models fail to leverage available monolingual data for syntactic transfer. Indo-European languages (Germanic, Romance, Slavic) transfer far better than Sino-Tibetan, Japonic, Koreanic, and Niger-Congo languages; ideogram scripts (Chinese, Japanese) underperform Latin script on NER. Models struggle to generalize to unseen POS tag n-grams and unseen named entities in target languages (large accuracy drops vs. seen combinations), and predictions are often inconsistent between English and translated versions of the same example (only ~68% consistency on XNLI). Machine-translation-based baselines (translate-train/-test) reduce but do not close the gap.

**Relevance:** Directly relevant to the interlingua hypothesis as an empirical stress test: if multilingual models truly learned shared, language-neutral representations, zero-shot transfer should be near-uniform across languages and tasks, but XTREME shows large, systematic gaps tied to language family, script, and task type (syntax/retrieval worst), plus inconsistent predictions across translated inputs — evidence that current models' "shared" representations are only partially language-neutral and skew toward Indo-European/Latin-script structure. Useful as a behavioral benchmark against which mechanistic interpretability work (e.g., probing for language-neutral subspaces or shared circuits) can be checked for consistency with observed transfer failures.

### Inducing Language-Agnostic Multilingual Representations

**File:** `Zhao-2020-Inducing-LanguageAgnostic-Multilingual-Reps.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Zhao, Eger, Bjerva, Augenstein, 2020 (arXiv:2008.09112, v2 2021)  
**Type:** paper

> Multilingual encoders like m-BERT and XLM-R encode strong "language identity" signals alongside content, and three complementary interventions (parallel-data re-alignment, batch normalization of embeddings, and typology-driven input normalization) can strip out much of that language-specific signal, narrowing (but not closing) the cross-lingual transfer gap.

**Problem:** Cross-lingual transfer with contextualized multilingual encoders (m-BERT, XLM-R) works well for high-resource languages typologically similar to English but degrades sharply for low-resource, typologically distant languages. The paper investigates why: it hypothesizes that these encoders retain language-specific ("language identity") information in their representations rather than producing truly language-agnostic ("interlingua-like") embeddings, and asks whether that signal can be removed to improve transfer, especially for dissimilar/low-resource languages.

**Method:** Three techniques, which can be combined additively: (1) JOINT-ALIGN — re-mapping vector spaces via fine-tuning that minimizes L2 distance between contextual embeddings of mutually-translated word pairs (extracted with FastAlign from Europarl/JW300 parallel corpora) while regularizing toward the original encoder state, orienting all languages toward English as pivot (Eq. 1-3, following Cao et al. 2020's joint re-mapping approach); (2) NORM — inserting a batch-normalization layer on the last-layer representations (zero mean, unit variance per batch, batch size 8), removing language-specific means/variances shown by prior work (Libovický et al. 2019) to encode language identity; contrasted with layer normalization, which performs worse due to zero-padding across variable sequence lengths; (3) TEXT — input-level normalization: removing morphological contractions (e.g., Italian "del" → "de il" via UDPipe tokenization) and reordering words (noun-adjective, subject-verb-object order) per WALS typological features, applied only to a smaller language sample since automatic typological feature extraction doesn't scale to all 19 languages. Evaluated on m-BERT and XLM-R across two tasks: zero-shot XNLI (natural language inference, transfer from English) and RFEval (reference-free MT evaluation via XMoverScore, using Earth Mover Distance between contextual embeddings), across 19 typologically diverse languages spanning 5 families (Austronesian, Germanic, Indo-Aryan, Romance, Uralic) with varying resource/similarity levels to English (Table 1). Analysis also uses t-SNE of language centroid embeddings and correlations (Kendall/Pearson/Spearman) between encoder-induced language similarity and WALS-based typological similarity.

**Findings:** Combining JOINT-ALIGN + NORM yields the largest gains: on RFEval, m-BERT improves by +10.1 points and XLM-R by +33.5 points on average; on XNLI, +7.6 and +2.8 points respectively (Table 2/3), and the gains are largely additive/superadditive rather than merely from either technique alone. NORM alone is simpler, cheaper, and more consistent across encoders/tasks than re-mapping (JOINT-ALIGN), and re-mapping is actually inconsistent for XLM-R on XNLI (sometimes slightly hurts, since XLM-R struggles to discriminate matched vs. random word-translation pairs, unlike m-BERT — shown via cosine-similarity histograms in Fig. 2). TEXT normalization (removing contractions, reordering adjectives/nouns or verbs/objects) gives smaller, inconsistent gains (e.g., helps French/Italian but not Spanish for RFEval/XNLI) and doesn't scale easily to many languages. Gains from combined methods are largest precisely for low-resource and typologically-distant-from-English languages (Fig. 9), directly narrowing (not closing) the cross-lingual transfer gap: overall averaged gap decreases by 8.9 points (m-BERT) and 18.2 points (XLM-R). t-SNE plots (Fig. 8) show language centroids cluster by family in the original space but this clustering diminishes after re-alignment/normalization, supporting that the modified representations are more language-agnostic; WALS-typology correlation with encoder-induced language similarity also drops after modification (Table 5), consistent with language-identity information being stripped out.

**Relevance:** Directly bears on the interlingua hypothesis: the paper provides empirical evidence that off-the-shelf multilingual encoders (m-BERT, XLM-R) do NOT spontaneously learn fully language-agnostic/interlingua representations — they retain measurable "language identity" signals (language-specific means/variances, poor discrimination between true translations and random word pairs) that correlate with typological distance (WALS features). It offers concrete mechanistic-adjacent probes (cosine similarity histograms of matched vs random pairs, t-SNE of language centroids, batch-norm as a literal "subtract the language mean" intervention) and shows that explicitly removing this signal narrows the transfer gap, especially for low-resource/dissimilar languages — useful as a contrast/complement to purely mechanistic-interp claims about shared multilingual subspaces or "shared semantic hub" behavior in modern LLMs.

### Information-Theoretic Probing for Linguistic Structure

**File:** `Pimentel-2020-InformationTheoretic-Probing.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Pimentel, Valvoda, Hall Maudslay, Zmigrod, Williams, Cotterell, 2020  
**Type:** paper

> Probing should be formalized as estimating the mutual information between a representation and a linguistic property, which implies one should always use the highest-performing probe available rather than a deliberately simple one — and under this framework, contextual embeddings like BERT provably cannot contain more information about a linguistic property than the original sentence.

**Problem:** The paper addresses what it means, formally, to "probe" a neural representation (e.g., BERT) for linguistic knowledge (e.g., part-of-speech, dependency labels). Prior work (esp. Hewitt and Liang 2019) assumed simpler probes are better because they avoid the probe just "memorizing" the task, and proposed "selectivity" (accuracy on real task minus accuracy on a randomized control task) as the criterion for good probe design. Pimentel et al. argue this intuition is confused and lacks a principled foundation.

**Method:** They operationalize probing as estimating the mutual information I(T;R) between a linguistic property T and a representation R, showing that H(T|R) can only be upper-bounded (giving a lower bound on I(T;R)) via the cross-entropy of a trained probe q_theta(t|r); the bound is tighter the better/more expressive the probe, formally justifying "always use the best probe you can train" instead of penalizing complexity. They introduce "control functions" c(·) = e∘id (a type-level/decontextualized lookup, e.g. fastText or one-hot embeddings composed with a function mapping a contextual embedding back to its word type) as an alternative to Hewitt & Liang's control tasks, and define the "gain" G(T,R,c) = I(T;R) - I(T;c(R)) as a variationally-bounded (upper and lower KL bounds), empirically estimable quantity measuring how much more information the contextual representation carries beyond a type-level baseline. They also prove analytically (via the data-processing inequality and the fact that BERT is a bijection between sentences and embedding sequences, under a mild uniqueness assumption) that I(T;BERT(S)) = I(T;S) — contextual embeddings cannot contain more task-relevant information than the raw sentence. Empirically, they train deep MLP probes (up to several layers, hyperparameter-searched over 50 models per estimate) on multilingual BERT representations (averaged wordpiece outputs) for POS tagging and dependency labeling across 11 typologically diverse languages (Basque, Czech, English, Finnish, Indonesian, Korean, Marathi, Tamil, Telugu, Turkish, Urdu) using Universal Dependencies 2.5 treebanks and the universal POS tagset, comparing MI estimates against fastText and one-hot control functions.

**Findings:** On POS tagging, BERT-based MI estimates exceed the fastText control in only 5 of 11 languages, and gains are modest (under 9%) even when positive — some languages show negative "gain" (an estimation artifact from imperfect probe/control approximation, not a true violation of the data-processing inequality). This suggests most POS information is already encoded at the lexical (type) level and sentential context adds little. On dependency labeling (a more context-dependent task), BERT improves over the type-level control in all 11 languages, but the maximum gain is only ~12% (in English, the highest-resource language); five languages show gains under 5%. The authors conclude multilingual BERT's representations do not encode dramatically more syntactic information than simple type-level embeddings, and that "ease of extraction" (how accessible information is to a probe), rather than raw information content, is the more meaningful thing to study going forward — since under their formalization, absolute information content is trivially bounded by the sentence itself.

**Relevance:** This paper is a key methodological/critical reference for anyone doing probing-based interpretability work on multilingual models, including tests of the interlingua hypothesis: it warns that a probing result showing "representation X encodes property Y" may mostly reflect probe capacity or lexical/type-level cues rather than genuine shared/contextual structure, and that naive MI/accuracy-based probing cannot, even in principle, reveal more linguistic structure than is already in the input sequence. For interlingua-hypothesis work specifically, its multilingual experimental setup (11 typologically diverse languages, comparing contextual vs. type-level control functions) is a template for rigorously separating "shared representation across languages" claims from confounds like lexical ambiguity or probe expressiveness, and its call to formalize "ease of extraction" foreshadows later minimum-description-length probing approaches (e.g., Voita and Titov 2020, cited in the paper) relevant to measuring how accessible/shared cross-lingual representations actually are.

### Finding Universal Grammatical Relations in Multilingual BERT

**File:** `Chi-2020-Universal-Grammatical-Relations-in-mBERT.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Chi, Hewitt, and Manning, 2020  
**Venue:** ACL 2020 (Proceedings of the 58th Annual Meeting of the ACL, pp. 5564-5577)  
**Type:** paper

> mBERT encodes syntactic dependency structure in a low-rank linear subspace that is largely shared across 11 typologically diverse languages, and the geometry of that subspace clusters head-dependent pairs into groups that closely (but imperfectly) track Universal Dependencies labels — even though the probe was never given label supervision.

**Problem:** Why is multilingual BERT (mBERT) so effective at zero-shot cross-lingual transfer (e.g., on XNLI, cross-lingual dependency parsing)? The authors ask specifically whether mBERT represents syntax in a way that is shared/universal across languages, rather than learning separate, language-private representations, and whether unsupervised structure in those representations aligns with linguists' notion of grammatical relations (dependency labels).

**Method:** They extend Hewitt & Manning's (2019) "structural probe": a linear transformation B (rank k, applied to mBERT's contextual embeddings h) is trained so that squared Euclidean distance ||Bh_i - Bh_j||^2 approximates the tree distance (number of edges) between words i,j in gold Universal Dependencies v2 parses. This defines a "syntactic subspace." They train/evaluate this probe on 11 languages (Arabic, Czech, German, English, Spanish, Farsi, Finnish, French, Indonesian, Latvian, Chinese) using BERT-Base Multilingual Cased (110M params), comparing against two baselines (MBERTRAND — randomly reinitialized attention layers, and LINEAR — a trivial left-to-right chain parse). Evaluation metrics are UUAS (undirected unlabeled attachment score, from a min spanning tree built from predicted distances) and DSpr. (Spearman correlation between predicted and gold word-pair distances). Beyond distance recovery, they conduct cross-lingual transfer experiments (train probe on language i, test on language j; hold-one-out; train on all languages jointly), measure subspace geometric similarity via principal angles between per-language B matrices, test extrapolation on prenominal vs. postnominal adjective ordering (using French, which has both), and — most novel — compute head-minus-dependent difference vectors v_diff = B(h_head - h_dep) for every dependency edge, then visualize them with t-SNE to see if they cluster in a way that reflects UD dependency labels, entirely without ever supervising the probe on labels.

**Findings:** The probe recovers syntax well in-language (avg. 22-point UUAS / 0.175 DSpr. improvement over baselines), with the syntactic subspace needing only ~64-128 of 768 dimensions and peaking at layers 7-8. Cross-lingual transfer works: a probe trained on the single best other language achieves +14 UUAS/+0.128 DSpr. over baseline; holding out the target language entirely (training on all others) still gives +16 UUAS/+0.137 DSpr.; a joint subspace trained on all languages together captures 88% of the possible UUAS improvement, evidence for a genuinely shared cross-lingual syntactic subspace. Subspace angle similarity between language pairs correlates strongly (Spearman ~0.78-0.82) with transfer performance. Adjective-order extrapolation to French shows probes generalize across prenominal/postnominal orderings regardless of training language's dominant order, suggesting non-superficial encoding. The t-SNE visualizations of difference vectors show mBERT's "native" clusters largely track UD labels across languages simultaneously (e.g., French/English dependencies of the same type co-cluster) but diverge from UD in linguistically interesting ways: adjectives split into prenominal/postnominal clusters rather than one amod class; determiners split into definite/indefinite/possessive/demonstrative sub-clusters; negatives get their own cluster distinct from advmod; relative pronouns and subordinating markers (nsubj vs. mark in UD) are merged into one cluster; expletive subjects cluster separately as in UD's expl.

**Relevance:** Directly evidences the interlingua hypothesis at the syntactic level: mBERT does not learn fully separate per-language grammars but a substantially shared, low-dimensional linear syntactic subspace, and the emergent (unsupervised) clustering of that subspace approximates a language-universal grammatical-relations taxonomy resembling but not identical to UD. Methodologically important for interpretability: it shows how a probe trained on one objective (tree distance) can be repurposed as a lens to discover unsupervised structure (dependency labels) the probe was never trained to predict — a useful pattern for other interlingua/shared-representation probing work. Also flags real limits (correlational not causal claims, confound with subword overlap per Singh et al. 2019, t-SNE interpretability caveats) that are relevant caveats for the class discussion.

### Bridging Linguistic Typology and Multilingual Machine Translation with Multi-View Language Representations

**File:** `Oncevay-2020-Bridging-Linguistic-Typology-and-MT.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Oncevay, Haddow &amp; Birch, 2020  
**Venue:** EMNLP 2020 (pages 2391-2406)  
**Type:** paper

> The paper fuses sparse, knowledge-base-derived linguistic typology vectors (WALS/URIEL) with dense NMT-learned language embeddings into a single shared vector space using singular vector canonical correlation analysis (SVCCA), and shows this fused space encodes both typological and genetic language relationships better than either source alone, with practical payoffs for multilingual NMT.

**Problem:** Linguistic typology knowledge bases (e.g. WALS, accessed via URIEL/lang2vec) give categorical, human-curated language features but are extremely sparse (mean 14% feature coverage per language across 2,679 languages). Meanwhile, dense language embeddings learned as a byproduct of multilingual tasks (e.g. NMT, language modeling) are continuous and data-driven but cover far fewer languages and their linguistic content is not well understood. The paper asks: can these two "views" of a language be combined to get the best of both (broad, structured typological knowledge + dense, task-learned signal), and what does each view actually encode when fused?

**Method:** They use SVCCA (singular vector canonical correlation analysis, adapting Raghu et al. 2017's technique from analyzing neural network layers to fusing language representations): first apply truncated SVD to each view separately to denoise/reduce redundancy (KB features are mostly one-hot; NMT embeddings are high-dimensional), then apply CCA to find a shared, correlation-maximizing projection of the two views. KB view: URIEL/lang2vec k-NN Syntax vectors (US, 103 WALS-derived binary features). Task-learned views: NMT-learned language embeddings from three multilingual many-to-English MT models — Bible corpus (LB, 512-dim, Malaviya et al. 2017, 731 languages, using an initial pseudo-token to mark source language), WIT3/TED-23 (LW, 256-dim, Tan et al. 2019, transformer, using "factored" embeddings concatenated per input token), and a new TED-53 embedding set (LT) they train themselves following Tan et al.'s factored-embedding approach on Qi et al. (2018)'s 53-language TED corpus. They evaluate the fused SVCCA representations on: (1) typological feature prediction via logistic regression in one-language-out and one-family-out settings; (2) phylogenetic tree reconstruction for 17 Indo-European languages, scored with a novel-to-the-task tree edit distance metric (APTED, normalized as nAPTED) against a lexicostatistic gold-standard tree; (3) two downstream multilingual NMT applications — language clustering (hierarchical agglomerative clustering, using Silhouette analysis instead of the ambiguous Elbow method to pick cluster count) and language ranking for transfer learning (comparing against the LANGRANK model of Lin et al. 2019). They train actual multilingual NMT systems (small Transformers, Marian NMT/Nematus toolkits, BPE via SentencePiece) on TED-53 to measure BLEU across these clustering/ranking schemes.

**Findings:** SVCCA-fused representations generally outperform single-view NMT-learned embeddings at predicting typological features (e.g. LW: 81.27%→84.83% accuracy one-language-out; LT: 77.96%→85.37%), except for the noisier Bible-derived LB embeddings (pseudo-token, not factored). For phylogeny reconstruction, fusing US (syntax) with LT via SVCCA gives the best match to the gold Indo-European tree (nAPTED 0.15 vs. 0.45 for KB alone or 0.26 for NMT alone), and a Spearman correlation analysis shows SVCCA(US,LT) correlates with the gold cophenetic distance matrix at 0.80, versus 0.48 for typology alone and 0.68 for NMT-learned alone — i.e., the fusion is more than additive. On downstream multilingual NMT: SVCCA-based clustering (10 clusters) matches or beats the massive (single model for all languages) and individual (pairwise) baselines in BLEU across resource bins, and crucially SVCCA lets new/unseen languages be projected into the shared space and clustered without retraining a massive model — a major practical advantage. For language ranking (choosing transfer/parent languages), SVCCA-based nearest-neighbor selection approximates the performance of the trained LANGRANK ranker without needing to pretrain a ranking model on pairwise MT results. A key methodological finding: language embeddings extracted using "factored" input representations (language embedding concatenated at every token) capture much more usable relational/typological signal than embeddings extracted via a simple initial pseudo-token, which produce embeddings with silhouette scores never exceeding 0.2 and no meaningful clustering structure.

**Relevance:** This is a pre-mechanistic-interpretability but directly relevant precedent for the "interlingua hypothesis": it empirically probes what relational/typological/genetic information is implicitly encoded in language-level embeddings learned incidentally by multilingual NMT models, and shows that such task-learned embeddings already correlate substantially (Spearman 0.68) with genetic/phylogenetic distance even without any typological supervision — consistent with multilingual models organizing languages by real linguistic structure rather than arbitrary task shortcuts. It also demonstrates concretely how architectural choices (factored vs. pseudo-token language conditioning) drastically change whether such shared/interlingua-like structure emerges at all, a methodological point relevant to designing or interpreting probes for shared multilingual representations in modern LLMs.

### What does it mean to be language-agnostic? Probing multilingual sentence encoders for typological properties

**File:** `Choenni-Shutova-2020-What-Does-It-Mean-LanguageAgnostic.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Rochelle Choenni and Ekaterina Shutova (2020)  
**Venue:** arXiv preprint (arXiv:2009.12862v1 [cs.CL], 27 Sep 2020); cited in the syllabus as COLING 2020, but no ACL Anthology COLING record exists for this title — this is the arXiv preprint version  
**Type:** paper

> Using classifier probes trained on WALS typological features, the paper shows that whether a multilingual sentence encoder becomes 'language-agnostic' (a shared interlingua) versus retains language-specific typological information depends systematically on its pretraining objective, with cross-lingual objectives trading away typological detail for universality.

**Problem:** While multilingual sentence encoders (LASER, M-BERT, XLM, XLM-R) transfer well across languages on downstream tasks, it is unclear what linguistic/typological properties of individual languages they actually encode, where in the network this information lives, and whether different pretraining strategies (monolingual vs. cross-lingual objectives) produce systematically different internal linguistic organization.

**Method:** The authors extract 25 typological features (word order, nominal/verbal morphology, negation, pronouns, etc.) from the WALS database, spanning 7 typologically diverse, related language pairs (e.g., Russian/Ukrainian, Hindi/Marathi, Italian/French) drawn from Tatoeba sentences (10K sentences/language). For each feature they train a lightweight one-layer MLP probing classifier (frozen encoder, macro-F1 scored, randomized-encoder baseline) to predict the language-level typological label from mean/max-pooled sentence representations, testing on the paired but held-out language to prevent the classifier from relying on language ID or lexical overlap. They probe both top-layer representations and every layer individually, plus use a Tenney-et-al.-style scalar layer-mixing technique (with KL-divergence from uniform as a localization metric) and t-SNE visualizations to see how/where typological information is distributed across layers.

**Findings:** All four encoders capture word order, pronoun, and negation properties, but M-BERT and XLM-R (monolingual MLM objectives) outperform LASER and XLM (cross-lingual MT/TLM objectives) on lexical and morphological features. In LASER and XLM, typological information is concentrated in lower layers and decays in higher layers (localizable), whereas in M-BERT/XLM-R it persists fairly uniformly across all layers. t-SNE shows LASER/XLM representations converge into a single shared cross-lingual space in top layers (consistent with an interlingua), while M-BERT/XLM-R representations re-separate into language-specific subspaces at higher layers — implying a negative correlation between a model's cross-lingual universality and its retention of language-specific typological information, regardless of architecture. XLM also generalizes reasonably well to typological features of languages unseen during its pretraining.

**Relevance:** Directly informs the interlingua hypothesis debate by showing that 'language-agnostic'/shared representations and richly encoded language-specific typological structure are in tension: cross-lingual training objectives push models toward a shared interlingua at the cost of typological detail, while monolingual objectives (M-BERT, XLM-R) preserve more language-specific structure but partition representation space by language rather than truly merging it. This gives mechanistic interpretability researchers concrete probing methodology (WALS-based typological probing tasks, layer-wise/scalar-mixing localization analysis) for testing claims about shared multilingual representations.

### Towards Monosemanticity: Decomposing Language Models With Dictionary Learning

**File:** `Bricken-2023-Towards-Monosemanticity.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Bricken, Templeton, Batson, et al. (Anthropic), 2023  
**Type:** paper

> Training a sparse autoencoder on a one-layer transformer's MLP activations extracts thousands of largely monosemantic, human-interpretable "features" from superposition, features that neurons themselves do not exhibit.

**Problem:** Individual neurons in language models are often polysemantic (respond to unrelated concepts, e.g. one neuron firing on academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests, and Korean text at once), making neuron-level analysis a poor basis for mechanistic interpretability. The hypothesized cause is "superposition": models represent more features than they have neurons by encoding each feature as a linear combination (near-orthogonal direction) over neurons, exploiting sparsity so multiple features can be packed into a lower-dimensional space (compressed-sensing-like). The paper asks whether a principled decomposition can recover the "true," more monosemantic features hidden in superposition.

**Method:** They train a one-layer transformer (512-neuron MLP, ReLU, trained on 100B tokens of The Pile) and then train sparse autoencoders (a single hidden ReLU layer plus linear decoder, with untied encoder/decoder weights, pre-encoder bias, dead-neuron "resampling," MSE reconstruction loss + L1 sparsity penalty on hidden activations) on 8 billion samples of that MLP's post-ReLU activations. Autoencoder widths range from 1x (512) to 256x (131,072) the neuron count, letting features decompose the MLP's activation vector into an overcomplete, sparse linear code x ≈ b + Σ f_i(x) d_i. They validate features via computational proxies (log-likelihood ratios for e.g. Arabic/Hebrew script, DNA, base64), causal ablation, logit-weight ("logit attribution") analysis, "pinned" feature sampling to steer generation, and cross-model universality checks (a second independently-seeded transformer, "B"). Interpretability is scored via blinded human rubric ratings and automated interpretability (Claude generating and testing explanations of feature activations/logit effects, following Bills et al.).

**Findings:** Features (e.g. an Arabic-script feature, a DNA feature, three distinct base64 sub-features, a Hebrew feature) are highly specific and sensitive to their hypothesized context, causally drive appropriate downstream token predictions upon ablation/pinning, do not correspond to any single neuron (correlations with best-matching neurons are weak, e.g. 0.09–0.2), and recur across independently trained models (activation correlations often >0.7–0.9), suggesting they are "real" and at least partly universal. At scale, humans and Claude-based auto-interpretability both rate features far more interpretable than neurons (median human rubric score 12 vs. 0 for neurons; 74% vs 58% logit-prediction accuracy). Larger dictionaries reveal "feature splitting" (single coarse features fracture into families of more specific ones) and features cluster in an "anisotropic" superposition geometry (not evenly spaced, as prior toy models assumed) tied to shared "action" effects on logits. Features chain into "finite-state-automata"-like systems (e.g. HTML tag generation, Unicode-continuation handling for scripts like Chinese/Tamil, apparent phrase memorization). The autoencoder features recover 79% of the MLP's log-likelihood loss contribution (up to 94.5% with more features/lower L1), though full "explaining the model" remains an open problem.

**Relevance:** Directly foundational to mechanistic interpretability method: establishes sparse-autoencoder dictionary learning as a way to find monosemantic, causally-validated units inside superposed activations, and empirically demonstrates cross-model feature universality (independently trained transformers converge on highly correlated features for the same concepts). This universality result is a close methodological cousin of the interlingua hypothesis — it shows models trained differently but on similar data converge on shared representational primitives, which is the same kind of evidence (convergent, reusable, model-independent features) that would be needed to argue multilingual models share language-neutral internal representations. The paper's language-related features (Arabic script, Hebrew script, French, German-detector) are also directly relevant test cases for how "language identity" is encoded as a feature in superposition.

### Toy Models of Superposition

**File:** `Elhage-2022-Toy-Models-of-Superposition.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Elhage, Hume, Olsson, Schiefer, Henighan, Kravec, Hatfield-Dodds, Lasenby, Drain, Chen, Grosse, McCandlish, Kaplan, Amodei, Wattenberg, Olah, 2022  
**Venue:** Transformer Circuits Thread (Anthropic)  
**Type:** paper

> Small ReLU networks trained on synthetic sparse data provably represent more "features" than they have neurons/dimensions by packing them non-orthogonally ("superposition"), and this phenomenon governs when neurons are cleanly monosemantic vs. messily polysemantic.

**Problem:** Individual neurons in real networks are sometimes cleanly interpretable (respond to one feature) but often "polysemantic" (respond to many unrelated things), especially in large language models. The paper asks why this happens, and specifically investigates whether networks can genuinely represent more features of the input than they have dimensions/neurons to store them in — and if so, under what conditions and with what structure.

**Method:** The authors build toy autoencoder-like models: a hidden-dimension vector x with n sparse, importance-weighted synthetic "features" is projected down via W into m < n dimensions (h = Wx) and reconstructed via a ReLU output (x' = ReLU(WᵀWx + b)). By sweeping feature sparsity (1−S), relative importance, and correlation structure, and visualizing WᵀW, feature norms ||Wᵢ||, and a "feature dimensionality" metric Dᵢ = ||Wᵢ||²/Σⱼ(Ŵᵢ·Wⱼ)², they empirically and analytically (via a Saxe-et-al.-style closed-form loss decomposition into "feature benefit" vs. "interference" terms, and a mapping to the Thomson problem) characterize when/how superposition emerges. They also test a "ReLU hidden layer" variant with a privileged basis (giving literal neurons) and an "absolute value" computation task to show superposition can support actual computation, not just storage.

**Findings:** Superposition is real: with dense features, ReLU output models behave like PCA/linear models and only represent as many features as dimensions, but as sparsity increases they pack many more features in as almost-orthogonal directions, tolerating interference. This transition is a sharp first-order phase change (features flip between "not represented," "dedicated dimension," and "superposition," and the phase boundary is analytically confirmed). Superposed features self-organize into specific geometric structures (antipodal pairs, triangles, pentagons, tetrahedra, square antiprisms) corresponding to solutions of the Thomson problem, with correlated features preferring local orthogonal bases (or collapsing into their PCA components) and anticorrelated features preferring antipodal placement. Networks can perform actual computation (e.g., absolute value via ReLU(x)+ReLU(−x)) while features remain in superposition, using motifs like "asymmetric superposition and inhibition." Superposition correlates with a >3x increase in adversarial vulnerability, tracking features-per-dimension. Learning dynamics show discrete "energy level" jumps and can be decomposed into sequential geometric transformations. The paper also lays out a strategic case that "solving superposition" (enumerating features) is central to interpretability-based AI safety claims, and outlines three approaches (avoid superposition, e.g. via L1 regularization or MoE; find an overcomplete basis post hoc via sparse coding/dictionary learning, which becomes the basis for later SAE work; or hybrid approaches).

**Relevance:** This is the foundational mechanistic-interpretability paper establishing that models represent more "concepts" than they have neurons by encoding them as near-orthogonal, non-basis-aligned directions in activation space — directly underpinning the "features as directions" / linear representation hypothesis that motivates the interlingua hypothesis (shared, language-neutral feature directions across languages). Its framework for when features get dedicated dimensions vs. share them via superposition, and its emphasis on correlated features being packed into local orthogonal or collapsed (PCA-like) subspaces, is the conceptual toolkit needed to reason about whether multilingual models encode language-agnostic concept directions or superpose language-specific and shared features together.

### ECLeKTic: a Novel Challenge Set for Evaluation of Cross-Lingual Knowledge Transfer

**File:** `Goldman-2025-ECLeKTic-Crosslingual-Knowledge-Transfer.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Goldman, Shaham, Malkin, Eiger, Hassidim, Matias, Maynez, Gilady, Riesa, Rijhwani, Rimell, Szpektor, Tsarfaty, Eyal (Google Research / Google DeepMind / Bar-Ilan University), 2025  
**Venue:** arXiv preprint (arXiv:2502.21228v3), Google Research / Google DeepMind, Nov 2025  
**Type:** paper

> Introduces ECLeKTic, a closed-book QA benchmark built from Wikipedia articles that exist in only one of 12 languages, which isolates true cross-lingual knowledge transfer (versus mere parallel exposure) and shows even top LLMs like Gemini 2.0 Pro transfer only ~65% of facts they can retrieve in the source language.

**Problem:** Prior work measuring "cross-lingual consistency" (whether a model gives the same answer to a translated question in different languages) cannot distinguish genuine shared, language-independent knowledge representations from the model having separately memorized the same fact in each language during pretraining (since translated benchmarks test facts likely seen in multiple languages). The paper asks: can we cleanly measure whether LLMs actually transfer factual knowledge acquired in one language to answer questions in another, in a black-box way that also works on closed proprietary models?

**Method:** The authors mined the July 2023 Wikipedia dump across 12 languages (English, French, German, Hebrew, Hindi, Indonesian, Italian, Japanese, Korean, Mandarin, Portuguese, Spanish) for articles that exist in exactly one language (>=200 chars, >=100 views in 2023) and have no equivalent in the other 11 — treating this asymmetry as a proxy for the fact being encoded in the model's weights only in that "source" language. Gemini generated candidate question/answer pairs from each such article; 27 human annotators (400 cumulative hours) verified, decontextualized (disambiguating locale-specific references), and translated each Q/A into all other languages (via English as pivot), producing 4,608 Q/A pairs (4,224 "target" cross-lingual examples + 384 source-language examples). They define two metrics: "overall success" (fraction of examples where the model answers correctly in both source and target language) and "transfer score" (conditional on answering correctly in the source language, fraction also answered correctly in the target language). Correctness is judged by an LLM-as-judge (Gemini 2.0 Flash checking against the source-language Wikipedia context) plus a word-recall string-matching metric as a cross-check. They evaluate 8 models (Gemini 2.0 Pro/Flash, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemma 2 9B, Mistral Nemo, Qwen 2.5 7B, Olmo 2 7B), analyze per-language-pair transfer (Figure 5), scaling behavior across the Qwen 2.5 model family (0.5B–72B), and run an ablation giving models increasing hints (general hint → source language name → source article title → full source-language context/open-book) to isolate whether failures are retrieval or reasoning failures.

**Findings:** Best model (Gemini 2.0 Pro) achieves only 41.6% overall success and 65.0% transfer score, meaning it fails to transfer roughly a third of facts it can correctly retrieve in the source language; open-source 7-9B models score far worse (transfer scores 17-40%). Shared script strongly eases transfer (Latin-script languages including genealogically unrelated Indonesian transfer well among each other; transfer between Chinese/Japanese kanji-sharing languages is also elevated), while script mismatch and specific languages (Hindi as target, Portuguese→Japanese at 23.5%) show much weaker transfer, and transfer is directionally asymmetric (Hindi as source: 78.6% avg; as target: 59.6%). Scaling Qwen 2.5 from 0.5B to 72B shows overall success keeps rising with scale, but transfer score saturates around 14B parameters — bigger models retrieve more facts in-language but don't proportionally get better at cross-lingual sharing. The hinting ablation shows simply telling the model to use other-language knowledge barely helps (+0.8pp), naming the source language helps more (+7pp), giving the article title helps further (+12pp), but giving the actual source-language context (open-book) nearly solves the task (94.3%) — indicating the bottleneck is cross-lingual retrieval of parametric knowledge, not the ability to reason across languages once the knowledge is available.

**Relevance:** Directly bears on the interlingua hypothesis: ECLeKTic is designed precisely to falsify naive claims of a shared language-neutral representation by controlling for parallel exposure, and its central finding — that transfer is partial, script-dependent, and scale-insensitive relative to raw retrieval — suggests current LLMs have only weak/partial interlingua-like sharing rather than a fully unified cross-lingual knowledge store. The open-book vs. closed-book ablation is especially useful for mech interp discussions: it localizes the failure to parametric knowledge retrieval/routing across language-specific representations rather than to cross-lingual reasoning per se, which is a useful behavioral finding to contrast with white-box studies (e.g., knowledge neurons, activation patching across languages) cited in their related work section.

### Scaling Monosemanticity: Extracting Interpretable Features from Claude 3 Sonnet

**File:** `Templeton-2024-Scaling-Monosemanticity.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Templeton, Conerly, Marcus, Lindsey, Bricken, et al. (Anthropic), 2024  
**Type:** paper

> Anthropic scaled sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to Claude 3 Sonnet, a full production LLM, and showed that the millions of resulting features are interpretable, causally steerable, and include highly abstract, multilingual and multimodal concepts.

**Problem:** Prior sparse-autoencoder work (Towards Monosemanticity, 2023) had only decomposed activations of a tiny one-layer transformer into interpretable "features," leaving open whether dictionary learning could scale to state-of-the-art production models and yield features useful for understanding and steering real-world model behavior, including safety-relevant behavior like deception, sycophancy, and bias.

**Method:** The authors train sparse autoencoders (SAEs) — a 2-layer encoder/decoder with ReLU and an L1 sparsity penalty on feature activations — on the residual stream activations at the middle layer of Claude 3 Sonnet. They train three SAEs of increasing size (~1M, ~4M, ~34M features) and use a scaling-laws analysis (compute-optimal tradeoff between number of features and training steps) to choose hyperparameters for the largest run. They then validate that features are interpretable via: (1) manual/automated specificity scoring using Claude 3 Opus rating activations on a 0–3 rubric, (2) causal "feature steering" experiments where individual features are clamped to high/low values and downstream generation is inspected, (3) comparison against MLP neurons showing features are far more interpretable/specific, (4) attribution and ablation analysis to find features acting as computational intermediates in multi-step reasoning, and (5) systematic feature-neighborhood/UMAP geometric analysis and "feature completeness" studies tying dictionary coverage to concept frequency in training data.

**Findings:** SAE reconstructions explain ≥65% of activation variance with <300 active features per token; larger SAEs have more "dead" features (2% at 1M, 65% at 34M) but capture rarer/finer-grained concepts via "feature splitting." Features are shown to be genuinely causal (not just correlational) — clamping features reliably induces corresponding behavior (e.g., a Golden Gate Bridge feature makes Claude self-identify as the bridge; a code-error feature makes Claude hallucinate or "fix" bugs; sycophancy, secrecy, bias, and dangerous-content features causally shift outputs). Features generalize remarkably: the Golden Gate Bridge feature fires on the same concept across many languages and on relevant images despite training only on text. The paper documents concrete safety-relevant feature families (unsafe code, backdoors, bias, sycophancy, deception/power-seeking, bioweapons, scam emails, self-representation) with steering demonstrations, while cautioning that feature existence doesn't imply the model is dangerous. Feature "completeness" analysis shows dictionary coverage of a concept follows a sigmoid function of that concept's frequency in training data, implying vastly larger SAEs (potentially billions of features) would be needed to capture very rare concepts.

**Relevance:** This paper is central evidence for mechanistic interpretability's feature/superposition framework at production scale and is directly relevant to the interlingua hypothesis: the Golden Gate Bridge and other features are explicitly shown to activate on the *same concept* across many different languages (English, Japanese, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese, Greek) and even across text/image modalities, suggesting Claude 3 Sonnet represents such concepts in a shared, language-neutral (and modality-neutral) direction rather than language-specific ones — a concrete mechanistic instantiation of a multilingual "interlingua" representation, though the authors note this generalization is only lightly investigated and left largely to future work.

### Towards Automated Circuit Discovery for Mechanistic Interpretability

**File:** `Conmy-2023-Automated-Circuit-Discovery-ACDC.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Conmy, Mavor-Parker, Lynch, Heimersheim, Garriga-Alonso, 2023  
**Type:** paper

> Introduces ACDC, an algorithm that automates the labor-intensive activation-patching step of mechanistic interpretability by recursively pruning edges from a model's computational graph to find a minimal circuit reproducing a target behavior.

**Problem:** Mechanistic interpretability circuit discovery (identifying which subgraph of a transformer's components — attention heads, MLPs — implements a specific behavior) has relied on tedious, manual activation-patching experiments by human researchers. This doesn't scale to larger models or more behaviors. The paper asks whether the "find connections between units" step of this workflow can be automated.

**Method:** The authors first formalize the mechanistic interpretability workflow as three steps: (1) choose a behavior, dataset, and metric; (2) define a computational DAG of the model at some granularity (e.g., attention heads/MLPs, optionally split by query/key/value or token position); (3) iteratively patch activations to prune unimportant components. ACDC automates step 3: starting from the full computational graph G, it processes nodes in reverse topological order (output to input) and, for each node, temporarily removes each incoming edge, replacing the ablated edge's activation with its value on a "corrupted" counterfactual input (interchange intervention), then measures the change in KL divergence between the full model's output and the pruned subgraph's output; if the KL divergence increase is below a threshold τ, the edge is permanently removed. They also adapt two baselines for the same task: Subnetwork Probing (SP, a gradient-based mask-learning method) and Head Importance Score for Pruning (HISP, gradient-attribution-based head ranking), generalizing both to use corrupted-activation ablation instead of zero-ablation. Evaluation uses ROC curves (edges classified as true/false positive relative to "canonical" circuits from prior manual work) across six tasks: IOI, Docstring, Greater-Than, two tracr-compiled toy tasks (xproportion, reverse), and Induction.

**Findings:** ACDC rediscovered all 5 component types of GPT-2 Small's IOI circuit, selecting only 68 of ~32,000 possible edges, all confirmed by prior manual work (Wang et al. 2023). Across tasks, ACDC achieves competitive-to-best AUC compared to SP and HISP on IOI, Greater-Than, and tracr-reverse, though it is not uniformly robust — performance is highly sensitive to the corrupted-data distribution, the metric optimized (KL divergence vs. task-specific metrics like logit difference), and hyperparameters. With zero-ablation instead of corrupted-activation ablation, ACDC perfectly recovers the toy tracr circuits. A key limitation: all methods tested optimize a single metric and systematically miss "negative" components (e.g., negative name-mover heads in IOI) unless thresholds are set very low. The paper also identifies a structural failure mode (Appendix M): ACDC and other methods fail to recover both inputs to OR-gate-like structures in the network, since greedily pruning one redundant input looks safe in isolation.

**Relevance:** This paper is foundational tooling for circuit-level mechanistic interpretability but is monolingual/task-behavior-focused (IOI, greater-than, induction, etc.) rather than about multilingual models or cross-lingual representations. Its relevance to the interlingua hypothesis is methodological: ACDC-style automated circuit discovery (activation patching + graph pruning via KL divergence thresholds) is a candidate tool for identifying whether a shared, language-neutral subcircuit performs a given task across languages, and its documented failure modes (missing negative/OR-gate components, high sensitivity to corrupted-data distribution choice) are important caveats for anyone applying automated circuit discovery to multilingual interlingua claims.

### Progress Measures for Grokking via Mechanistic Interpretability

**File:** `Nanda-2023-Progress-Measures-for-Grokking.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Nanda, Chan, Lieberum, Smith, Steinhardt, 2023  
**Type:** paper

> Reverse-engineers a one-layer transformer trained on modular addition to discover it implements a "Fourier multiplication" algorithm (embed numbers as rotations, add via trig identities, read off via constructive interference), and uses this understanding to construct progress measures showing grokking is actually gradual circuit formation followed by abrupt weight-decay-driven cleanup of memorization.

**Problem:** Neural networks sometimes show "emergent" abrupt capability jumps (e.g., grokking: a model trained on a modular-addition task overfits the training set for thousands of epochs, then suddenly generalizes to near-perfect test accuracy). The paper asks whether such apparently discontinuous phase transitions are actually underlain by continuous, measurable progress, and proposes that mechanistic interpretability — fully reverse-engineering the algorithm a network has learned — can be used to derive such "progress measures."

**Method:** They train a 1-layer ReLU transformer (d_model=128, 4 heads, 512 MLP neurons, no LayerNorm, untied embed/unembed) on a mod P=113 addition task with 30% of all input pairs, AdamW with weight decay λ=1, for 40k epochs, and observe consistent grokking. They reverse-engineer the learned weights via four lines of evidence: (1) periodicity of embeddings/activations/logits under Fourier transform, revealing 5 "key frequencies"; (2) showing the unembed-output matrix W_L is rank-10 and decomposable into sin/cos of the key frequencies, with MLP activations linearly encoding cos(w_k(a+b)) and sin(w_k(a+b)); (3) showing individual attention heads and MLP neurons are well-approximated by degree-2 polynomials of a single frequency; (4) ablation studies confirming these components are causally load-bearing (ablating key frequencies destroys performance; ablating everything else improves it slightly). From this mechanistic understanding they define two progress measures — restricted loss (keep only key-frequency Fourier components) and excluded loss (remove only key-frequency components) — plus a Gini-coefficient sparsity measure and the L2 norm of weights, and track these across training.

**Findings:** The reverse-engineered "Fourier multiplication algorithm" fits the network extremely well (e.g., 93-98% variance explained per frequency, 95% overall logit variance explained by 5 frequencies out of a 113×113×113-dimensional output space), and ablation confirms causal necessity of exactly the 5 key frequencies. Using the progress measures, training splits into three continuous phases: memorization (epochs 0-1.4k, model fits train data using frequencies unrelated to the final solution), circuit formation (epochs 1.4k-9.4k, the generalizing Fourier circuit gradually strengthens while train/test loss stay flat — this happens well before grokking is visible), and cleanup (epochs 9.4k-14k, weight decay abruptly removes the now-unneeded memorization components, causing the sudden test-accuracy jump traditionally called "grokking"). They show grokking requires both weight decay (or other regularization like dropout, but not L1) and a limited-data regime, and replicate the three-phase pattern across other seeds, architectures, prime moduli, and even non-arithmetic tasks (5-digit addition, repeated-subsequence prediction, skip-trigrams).

**Relevance:** This is a canonical, foundational mechanistic interpretability case study demonstrating full reverse-engineering of a transformer's learned algorithm at the level of individual weights/neurons, and shows how a network can encode task-relevant information in a highly structured, sparse basis (here, a Fourier/frequency basis rather than the neuron basis) — directly relevant to the interlingua hypothesis's core question of whether/how multilingual models might encode information in a shared, basis-independent, language-neutral representation rather than in surface (e.g., token or language) directions. It's a template for how one might look for and validate a purported shared interlingua representation via linear probing, ablation, and reconstruction fidelity metrics, and illustrates that "emergent" abrupt behavioral shifts can mask continuous underlying representational change — a caution relevant to interpreting sudden shifts in cross-lingual transfer or alignment.

### Language models can explain neurons in language models

**File:** `Bills-2023-LMs-Can-Explain-Neurons-in-LMs.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Bills, Cammarata, Mossing, Tillman, Gao, Goh, Sutskever, Leike, Wu, Saunders (OpenAI), 2023  
**Type:** paper

> OpenAI uses GPT-4 itself to automatically generate, simulate, and quantitatively score natural-language explanations of what causes individual neurons in GPT-2 to activate, scaling interpretability to hundreds of thousands of neurons.

**Problem:** Understanding a language model at the level of individual neurons/circuits does not scale — manual circuit analysis (e.g. IOI circuit work) only covers a handful of behaviors, but a model like GPT-2 XL has 307,200 MLP neurons. The paper asks whether we can automate the process of explaining what a neuron represents, and whether we can define a quantitative, scalable metric of "interpretability" rather than relying on qualitative human judgment.

**Method:** A three-model pipeline: (1) an explainer model (GPT-4) is shown (token, activation) pairs from 5 top-activating 64-token text excerpts (activations normalized/discretized 0-10) and produces a short natural-language hypothesis about what makes the neuron fire; (2) a simulator model (GPT-4) is conditioned on that explanation and predicts, token-by-token, what the neuron's activation would be on held-out excerpts (via an "all at once" method that reads token logprobs in parallel rather than one forward pass per token); (3) an automatic scorer compares simulated vs. real activations using the Pearson correlation coefficient (linearly calibrated), yielding an "explanation score" in roughly [0,1], validated against a more expensive ablation-based score (Jensen-Shannon divergence of model outputs when neuron is ablated to simulated vs. mean value) and against human explanation-ranking judgments. They test on GPT-2 (subject models 98K–6.7B params) using WebText excerpts, and explore improvements: iterative explanation revision (generating adversarial counterexample sentences and re-explaining), scaling explainer/simulator model capability, next-token-conditioned explanations, unigram/token-lookup baselines, changing subject-model activation functions (ReLU + sparsity), and an optimization method to find more "explainable" linear combinations of neurons (directions) via coordinate ascent on the score.

**Findings:** Average GPT-2 explanation scores are low in absolute terms (top-and-random: 0.151; random-only: 0.037), and only ~1.7% of 307,200 neurons scored above 0.7; the typical neuron is polysemantic and not well captured by a short explanation. Explanation/simulation quality scales with the capability of the explainer and simulator models (GPT-4 best, approaching but not matching human contractor performance/agreement). Iterative revision (sourcing adversarial sentences and re-explaining) measurably improves scores by fixing overly-broad explanations. Scores decline in later layers and in larger models, though this may partly be a limitation of the method rather than the models becoming less interpretable. Sparse/ReLU activation functions improve explainability at a cost to pretraining loss. Optimized "virtual neuron" directions (linear combos of neurons via gradient ascent, layer 10 of GPT-2 small) reach avg score 0.718 vs. 0.147 for actual neurons, suggesting more explainable directions exist beyond the neuron basis — a signal relevant to superposition. They open-sourced the GPT-2 XL explanation dataset, code, and a neuron viewer.

**Relevance:** Directly relevant to the mech interp toolkit portion of the seminar: it is a foundational automated-interpretability method (explain → simulate → score) that could in principle be applied cross-lingually to test whether "language-neutral" neurons/directions in multilingual models have consistent explanations across languages — e.g., running the pipeline on multilingual text excerpts to see if the same neuron's explanation is language-agnostic could operationalize the interlingua hypothesis. It's also a case study in the polysemanticity/superposition problem (directions vs. neurons) that is central to interpreting multilingual shared representations, and its "explainable directions" optimization foreshadows sparse-autoencoder-style feature extraction that later work applies to multilingual interlingua analysis.

### Representation Engineering: A Top-Down Approach to AI Transparency

**File:** `Zou-2023-Representation-Engineering.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Zou, Phan, Chen, Campbell, Guo, Ren, et al. (Center for AI Safety, CMU, UC Berkeley, Stanford et al.), 2023  
**Venue:** arXiv:2310.01405 (v4, March 2025)  
**Type:** paper

> Proposes "representation engineering" (RepE) — a top-down alternative to mechanistic interpretability that treats population-level activation directions (rather than individual neurons/circuits) as the unit of analysis for reading and controlling high-level concepts (honesty, morality, utility, power-seeking, emotion, harmlessness) in LLMs.

**Problem:** Mechanistic interpretability's bottom-up focus on neurons and circuits (the "Sherringtonian" view) struggles to scale to complex, high-level cognitive phenomena in LLMs and requires heavy manual circuit-tracing effort. The paper asks whether a "Hopfieldian" view — treating representations/activation spaces as the primary unit of analysis, as in cognitive neuroscience — can give more tractable, scalable traction on understanding and controlling safety-relevant model behaviors like honesty, deception, and power-seeking.

**Method:** RepE has two components. (1) Representation Reading, via a baseline called Linear Artificial Tomography (LAT): design contrastive stimulus/task templates (e.g., "pretend you're a dishonest/honest person..."), collect the model's hidden-state activations at a chosen token position (e.g., last token, or the concept token) across layers, then fit a linear model — primarily unsupervised PCA on pairwise difference vectors (also compared against K-means, mean-difference, logistic regression, prompt-difference) — to obtain a single "reading vector" per layer that predicts the target concept via a dot product with activations. (2) Representation Control: use these vectors (or stimulus-dependent "contrast vectors," or low-rank adapters trained with a contrastive loss — LoRRA, Low-Rank Representation Adaptation) to causally steer generation, via operations like linear combination (add/subtract the vector to activations), piecewise (conditional) operations, or projection (removing a concept's component). Evaluation follows a four-tier framework borrowed from neuroscience: correlation (does it predict the concept), manipulation (does steering it change behavior), termination (lesion — does removing it degrade performance), and recovery (does reintroducing it restore performance). Case studies extract directions for truthfulness/honesty (using TruthfulQA, ARC, CommonsenseQA, RACE, OpenBookQA, and statements from Azaria & Mitchell 2023), utility and morality (ETHICS dataset), power-aversion (a power ontology dataset from Pan et al. 2023 based on French's 1959 bases of power), emotion, harmlessness, bias/fairness, knowledge editing, and memorization.

**Findings:** LAT reading vectors substantially outperform zero-shot and few-shot baselines at detecting truthfulness/correctness across five QA benchmarks, and on TruthfulQA MC1 achieve state-of-the-art results, improving 18.1 percentage points over zero-shot accuracy (e.g., LLaMA-2-Chat 13B rises from ~35.9% standard zero-shot to ~69.8% with LAT, and control methods like the Contrast Vector or LoRRA push accuracy to 47.9–54.0%, approaching GPT-4-level performance on this benchmark despite being far smaller). The paper demonstrates a consistent, generalizable internal "concept of truth" that is distinct from surface output — models can internally represent a true answer while emitting a false one (dishonesty vs. incapability), enabling a token-level lie/hallucination detector built by summing honesty scores across middle layers. Manipulation experiments show adding/subtracting honesty vectors reliably flips a model between truthful and deceptive outputs, establishing causal (not just correlational) control. Similar reading/control pipelines successfully extract and steer utility judgments (ETHICS Utilitarianism task), commonsense morality, and power-seeking/power-aversion tendencies, with detectors activating when a model contemplates coercive or violent actions. Among linear-model variants, unsupervised PCA and K-means and supervised mean-difference are most robust across correlation/manipulation/termination tests, while logistic-regression directions correlate well but have little causal effect, and prompt-difference directions manipulate well but aren't necessary (poor termination) — underscoring that correlation alone is an unreliable indicator of a concept's causal role.

**Relevance:** This paper is central methodological infrastructure for interlingua-style multilingual interpretability work: LAT-style contrastive reading vectors and linear-combination steering are the standard toolkit used in later papers probing whether multilingual models encode language-neutral, high-level concepts (truthfulness, sentiment, etc.) in a shared representational subspace. Its "top-down"/Hopfieldian framing — concepts as directions in activation space rather than localized circuits — is exactly the lens needed to ask whether an interlingua representation exists and is causally steerable across languages, and its four-tier evaluation framework (correlation/manipulation/termination/recovery) offers a rigorous template for testing cross-lingual causal claims about shared representations.

### A Primer in BERTology: What We Know About How BERT Works

**File:** `Rogers-2020-Primer-in-BERTology.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Rogers, Kovaleva, and Rumshisky, 2020  
**Type:** survey

> A systematic survey of 150+ "BERTology" studies synthesizing what probing, attention-analysis, ablation, and compression research has revealed about how BERT represents linguistic/world knowledge, where that knowledge lives in the architecture, and how overparameterized the model is.

**Problem:** Despite BERT's dominance as an NLP baseline, it's unclear why it works so well; the paper asks what has been empirically learned about what knowledge BERT encodes, where/how that knowledge is localized in its layers and attention heads, and how much of its capacity is actually necessary.

**Method:** The authors synthesize over 150 prior empirical studies organized around three families of analysis technique: (1) fill-in-the-gap MLM probing (masking tokens and checking predictions, e.g. Petroni et al.'s LAMA-style knowledge-base probes, Ettinger's psycholinguistic diagnostics), (2) probing classifiers and structural probes trained on top of frozen BERT representations (e.g. Hewitt & Manning's structural probe recovering syntax trees, Tenney et al.'s edge probing), and (3) self-attention weight analysis (classifying attention head "patterns" — vertical, diagonal, block, heterogeneous — per Kovaleva et al. 2019, and testing whether heads track syntactic dependencies like obj/nsubj). They also review architecture/pretraining-objective ablations (varying heads vs. layers vs. hidden size; masking strategies; NSP alternatives) and pruning/compression studies (lottery ticket subnetworks, distillation, quantization) as complementary evidence about what's causally used at inference time versus merely present.

**Findings:** BERT encodes substantial syntactic knowledge (hierarchical structure recoverable from token embeddings, especially in middle layers — layers 6-9 of BERT-base per Hewitt & Manning) but this is not directly legible in self-attention weights, and no single head contains a full parse tree. It captures semantic roles, entity types, and can act as a competitive knowledge base for some relation types (Petroni et al.), yet fails at multi-step reasoning, negation, numeracy, and is brittle to shuffled/malformed input and named-entity substitution. Most self-attention heads (fewer than 50% show interpretable "heterogeneous" patterns) do not encode non-trivial linguistic information and are redundant — Voita et al. and Michel et al. show most heads can be pruned with little performance loss, and 30-40% of weights can be pruned overall (Gordon et al.), pointing to substantial overparameterization. The final layers are the most task-specific (change most during fine-tuning), while middle layers are most transferable across tasks.

**Relevance:** This is a foundational interpretability/probing methodology survey rather than a multilingual paper, but it is directly relevant background for the interlingua hypothesis discussion: it establishes the core toolkit (probing classifiers, structural probes, attention-head analysis, amnesic/information-theoretic probing, pruning-as-analysis) that multilingual interpretability work borrows to ask whether shared representations across languages are causally used versus merely present, and its cautionary methodological points (probes may inject their own knowledge, absence of a pattern doesn't prove absence of the capability, ablation studies assume localized rather than diffuse encoding) apply directly to claims about "shared, language-neutral" subspaces in multilingual models.

### Toward Transparent AI: A Survey on Interpreting the Inner Structures of Deep Neural Networks

**File:** `Rauker-2023-Toward-Transparent-AI-Survey.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Tilman Räuker, Anson Ho, Stephen Casper, Dylan Hadfield-Menell, 2023 (arXiv v6, Aug 2023; orig. 2022)  
**Venue:** arXiv preprint (arXiv:2207.13243v6), cs.LG  
**Type:** survey

> A taxonomy-driven survey of over 300 works on "inner interpretability" — methods that explain a DNN's weights, neurons, subnetworks, or internal representations — arguing the field currently produces mostly unfalsifiable hypotheses rather than engineering-useful tools.

**Problem:** DNNs are black boxes deployed in high-stakes settings; test-set performance doesn't guarantee sound learned solutions, doesn't reveal harmful biases/deception, and doesn't help fix bugs. The authors want to organize the sprawling, fast-growing literature on "inner interpretability" (methods that explain internal structure, as opposed to input attribution or black-box methods) into a coherent taxonomy, and to diagnose why the field has largely failed to produce practically useful tools for engineers despite hundreds of papers.

**Method:** Not empirical — a structured literature review/taxonomy paper. They classify ~300+ methods along two axes: (1) which part of the computational graph is explained — weights, individual neurons, subnetworks, or latent representations (Sections II–V) — and (2) whether the method is intrinsic (built in during training, e.g. continual learning, modularity, disentanglement, self-explaining architectures) or post hoc (applied after training, e.g. dataset-based neuron characterization/"dissection," feature synthesis, ablation/perturbation, gradient attribution, probing, concept vectors, representation comparison, circuits analysis). They also catalog "hazards" specific to each level (frivolous weights/neurons, polysemantic neurons) and survey cross-cutting connections to adversarial robustness, continual learning, modularity, network compression, and the human visual system.

**Findings:** Key claimed connections: interpretable models tend to be more adversarially robust and vice versa; adversarial training improves transfer learning and representation quality; polysemantic neurons (activated by multiple unrelated features) become more common over training and may reflect efficient "superposition" of features, posing a core obstacle to neuron-level interpretation; circuits analysis and mechanistic interpretability have so far only succeeded on toy models with heavy manual expert effort (e.g. curve detectors, induction heads); probing is versatile but methodologically fragile (successful probes don't imply the model uses that info; failed probes don't imply absence). Central discussion-section argument: interpretability work chronically mistakes plausible hypotheses for validated conclusions, over-relies on cherry-picked best-case examples, rarely tests at scale beyond toy/MNIST-level models, and needs rigorous benchmarks (e.g., planting-and-rediscovering-flaws competitions) plus a shift toward diagnostics/debugging-oriented, application-focused research.

**Relevance:** This is core background reading for the seminar: it establishes the vocabulary/taxonomy (intrinsic vs. post hoc; weights/neurons/subnetworks/representations) and the field's methodological standards that any interlingua-hypothesis work on multilingual LLMs would be judged against. It directly covers probing (Section V-F) and representation comparison (Section V-G, CCA/CKA/SVCCA/model stitching) — the exact toolkit used to test whether multilingual models share a language-neutral latent space — and flags their known pitfalls (probe validity, disagreement between similarity metrics), which are essential caveats for evaluating interlingua claims. It also surfaces the "convergent learning" hypothesis (Sec. VII, citing Wentworth's "natural abstraction hypothesis") — that different models/architectures learn similar representations — which is conceptually the same claim as the interlingua hypothesis applied across languages within one model.

### Mechanistic Interpretability: On the Internals of LLMs

**File:** `MI-Tutorial-Pranav-SLIDES.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Pranav (course instructor/presenter), n.d. — synthesizes work primarily by Chris Olah, Nelson Elhage, and other Anthropic/Distill researchers (2017-2023)  
**Type:** slide deck

> A tutorial deck surveying the core conceptual toolkit of mechanistic interpretability (MI) — features, circuits, the residual stream, privileged bases, and superposition — using Anthropic/Distill research as its worked examples.

**Problem:** LLMs are ubiquitous but trained via processes (unsupervised pretraining, SFT, RLHF) that don't inherently produce trustworthy or transparent systems, raising concerns about hallucination and malicious outputs. Existing analysis methods (input-output maps, CAMs, attention visualizations) treat models as black boxes rather than explaining the internal computations that produce behavior. The deck asks: how do we understand what a model's internal computations actually mean?

**Method:** The deck builds up MI's conceptual vocabulary step by step, grounded in the "Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits" (Olah et al., 2020) framing of reverse-engineering networks. Key building blocks: (1) "Features" — human-interpretable tensors/directions in a model's activation space, defined operationally by the input that maximally activates them (illustrated with CNN feature visualization and Bricken et al.'s 2023 "Towards Monosemanticity" language examples, e.g. steering a model's script output via feature interventions on Han Chinese, base64, DNA, hexadecimal directions). (2) "Circuits" — subgraphs connecting features across layers, not necessarily aligned to architectural boundaries. (3) The "activation space" (an n-dimensional vector space per layer) and "privileged basis" — the idea that individual neurons only carry clean, human-interpretable meaning when architectural/training peculiarities (e.g., sigmoid nonlinearities pushing activations to hypercube vertices) align features with the neuron basis. (4) Elhage et al.'s (2021) "Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits," which reframes the transformer around a "residual stream": attention heads read/write to it via W_V/W_O ("output-value" circuit) and decide what to read/write via W_Q/W_K ("query-key" circuit); attention heads move information across token positions while MLPs move information across dimensions within a token's stream. Under simplifying assumptions (no MLPs, no LayerNorm — "pseudo-linearized attention"), head-to-head interactions become tractable matrix products. (5) The "Toy Model" methodology: probe a large model for a behavior, build the minimal architecture reproducing it, then analyze that toy model — demonstrated via "induction heads," paired attention heads (needing ≥2 layers) that complete repeated patterns ([a][b]...[a]→[b]) and are hypothesized to underlie in-context learning. (6) "Superposition" (Elhage et al. 2022, "Toy Models of Superposition") — the phenomenon where models embed more features than they have dimensions, exploiting near-orthogonality of high-dimensional random vectors (Johnson–Lindenstrauss) and L1-style sparse reconstruction (Candès–Tao) so that sparse features can still be recovered despite interference/polysemanticity.

**Findings:** The deck doesn't present novel experimental results; it synthesizes established findings: neuron activations are not reliably privileged/monosemantic due to superposition, especially in MLP layers, which is identified as the central roadblock for standard MI methods since it prevents finding a "clean" feature basis. Induction heads are highlighted as a validated, well-understood circuit that supports the residual-stream-as-information-highway framing and is linked to in-context learning across model scales. The 62-page "Toy Models of Superposition" paper is cited as having developed a fuller theory covering the high-to-low-dimensional mapping, topology of superposed representations, computation across superposed subspaces, and effects on learning dynamics.

**Relevance:** This deck is purely foundational/methodological — it establishes the vocabulary (features, circuits, residual stream, privileged basis, superposition) that any interlingua-hypothesis discussion will presuppose, but it contains no multilingual content, no interlingua discussion, and no cross-lingual representation analysis. Its only near-multilingual moment is the Bricken et al. example showing script/language-switching features (Han Chinese, Arabic, Hebrew, base64, DNA) as illustrations of "language features," which is suggestive of shared representational machinery across scripts/languages but is not developed into any interlingua claim. Treat this as prerequisite background reading, not as evidence for or against the interlingua hypothesis.

### Grokking: Generalization Beyond Overfitting on Small Algorithmic Datasets

**File:** `Power-2022-Grokking.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Power, Burda, Edwards, Babuschkin (OpenAI), Misra (Google), 2022  
**Type:** paper

> Small transformers trained on algorithmic binary-operation tables (e.g. modular arithmetic, permutation group composition) can suddenly jump from memorized-but-non-generalizing to near-perfect validation accuracy long after training accuracy has saturated — a phenomenon the authors dub "grokking."

**Problem:** How overparameterized neural networks generalize beyond the point of memorizing/interpolating their training set — a poorly understood aspect of deep learning generalization theory. The authors want a small, cheap, controllable testbed (reproducible on a single GPU) to study data efficiency, memorization vs. generalization, and speed of learning in fine detail, rather than relying on large natural-data experiments.

**Method:** They construct datasets of equations a∘b=c for binary operations over p=97 (e.g. modular addition, subtraction, division, x²+y², x²+xy+y², cubic polynomials) and over the symmetric group S5 (composition, conjugation), presenting each element as an unstructured, arbitrary discrete token (no numeric/positional structure given to the model) so the network must infer relational structure purely from interaction patterns — like filling in a Sudoku-style operation table. A fixed random fraction of the table is used for training, the rest for validation. They train a small decoder-only transformer (2 layers, width 128, 4 heads, causal masking, ~4×10^5 non-embedding params), mostly with AdamW (lr 1e-3, weight decay 1, minibatch 512, up to 10^5–10^6 optimization steps), computing loss/accuracy only on the answer token. They sweep training-data fraction, optimizer/regularization variants (full-batch vs. minibatch Adam, dropout, weight decay toward origin vs. initialization, gradient/weight noise, learning rate), inject label-noise outliers, run t-SNE on learned output-embedding matrices, and correlate validation accuracy with a sharpness measure (Keskar et al. 2016) via Spearman correlation.



**Findings:** On modular division (mod 97, 50% train data), training accuracy hits ~100% within <10^3 steps while validation accuracy stays at chance until ~10^5 steps, then rapidly reaches ~100% by ~10^6 steps — a validation loss "double descent" that occurs far after training loss has already converged (Fig. 1, Fig. 4). Optimization steps needed to reach 99% validation accuracy grow sharply as training-data fraction shrinks (e.g., near 25-30% data, a 1% data decrease costs 40-50% more steps), while steps-to-fit-training-data stays roughly flat (10^3-10^4). Weight decay (AdamW) is the single most effective intervention, more than halving the data needed for generalization; other stochasticity (minibatch noise, gradient noise, weight noise) also helps, consistent with a flat-minima account. Symmetric operations (x+y, x·y) generalize with less data than asymmetric counterparts (x−y, x/y); some operations (e.g., x³+xy²+y mod 97) never generalize within budget — networks purely memorize. t-SNE of output embeddings reveals interpretable structure (circular topology for modular addition, coset structure of a subgroup for S5) especially with weight decay. Sharpness of the found minimum correlates strongly with validation accuracy (Spearman ρ = −0.795, p<0.000014) on the S5 task, suggesting grokking coincides with settling into flatter loss regions. A modest number of label-noise outliers (up to ~1000) barely hurts generalization, implying model capacity is not the bottleneck.

**Relevance:** This is a canonical mechanistic-interpretability touchstone: grokking motivated a wave of circuit-level analysis (e.g., Nanda et al.'s modular-addition work) showing that the sudden generalization jump corresponds to the network discovering a clean, structured algorithm (e.g., Fourier/circular embeddings) that replaces memorization. For the interlingua hypothesis, the paper's t-SNE finding — that networks trained on discrete, unstructured symbols spontaneously recover the latent algebraic structure (group/ring topology) connecting those symbols — is a direct small-scale analogue of the claim that multilingual models discover a shared, language-neutral internal structure/representation from surface-form tokens alone, useful as a minimal model for discussing how and when models internalize abstract shared structure versus memorizing surface mappings.

### Transformers learn factored representations

**File:** `arXiv-2602.02385-Factored-Representations.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Shai, Amdahl-Culleton, Christensen, Bigelow, Rosas, Boyd, Alt, Ray, Riechers, 2026  
**Type:** paper

> Transformers pretrained on next-token prediction have an inductive bias to represent conditionally-independent latent "parts" of the data-generating process in separate orthogonal linear subspaces of the residual stream (a direct-sum/factored geometry), rather than in one exponentially larger joint tensor-product space — even when that costs predictive accuracy.

**Problem:** The paper asks whether and how transformers, trained only on flat streams of tokens with no explicit signal about compositional structure, discover that the world underlying the data is made of separate "parts" (factors), and what geometric form that discovered representation takes internally.

**Method:** Building on belief-state geometry work (Shai et al. 2024), the authors formalize training data as generalized Hidden Markov Models (GHMMs) and define "predictive vectors" (the belief state needed for optimal next-token prediction). They prove that when a GHMM factors into conditionally-independent parts (transition operator = tensor product of per-factor operators), predictive vectors stay on a low-dimensional "product-state manifold" and there exists a lossless linear map from this manifold to a factored, direct-sum representation using only Σ(d_n−1) dimensions instead of the exponentially larger Π(d_n)−1 joint dimension. They call the claim that transformers actually adopt this native, linearly-accessible factored geometry (even when capacity allows the joint alternative) the "Factored World Hypothesis" (FWH), with three testable predictions: reduced dimensionality, orthogonal per-factor subspaces, and preference for factoring over fidelity. They test this on small GPT-2-style transformers (4 layers, d_model 120–480) trained with next-token cross-entropy on synthetic sequences composed of 5 factors (three "Mess3" 3-state HMMs + two "Bloch Walk" 3-D GHMMs, from prior work), whose sub-tokens are combined into a single 433-token vocabulary so no factor structure is visible at the token level. Three regimes are tested: fully independent factors, a conditionally-independent dependency chain, and "indecomposable" generators corrupted by a noisy channel (parameter ε) that breaks true factorizability. Analysis tools: cumulative explained variance (CEV) via PCA on residual-stream activations, linear regression from activations to ground-truth predictive vectors (RMSE), a "vary-one" subspace-identification procedure, and a principal-angle-based subspace overlap metric.

RNNs and LSTMs are also tested for comparison.

**Findings:** For independent and conditionally-independent factors, activations compress to ~10-11 effective dimensions (matching the factored prediction of Σ(d_n−1)=10) rather than the ~135-242 dimensions the joint/tensor-product representation would require, and this holds even for models with d_model up to 480 (far exceeding what's needed for the joint form) and for longer contexts (L=33,101). Factor subspaces become approximately orthogonal over training (subspace overlap metric drops from near-1 at init to near-0), and factored structure is already visible in the learned token-embedding matrix (~12 non-negligible singular values) before any transformer layers. Critically, in the noisy/indecomposable regime (factors corrupted with probability ε), the model still rapidly collapses to the ~10-dimensional factored representation within hundreds of gradient steps regardless of ε, even though this is provably lossy, and only later — under sustained loss pressure — gradually expands dimensionality to recover fidelity; the factored solution behaves as a "representational attractor." RNNs and LSTMs show qualitatively similar (though less sharp) factoring behavior, suggesting some architecture-generality.

**Relevance:** Directly relevant to the interlingua hypothesis and mech interp discussions of shared/universal representations: it provides a rigorous, falsifiable theoretical and empirical framework (rooted in belief-state/predictive-state geometry) for why and how neural nets factor a data-generating process into separable, low-dimensional, linearly-readable orthogonal subspaces — a concrete mechanistic story for "parts"/disentangled or possibly language-neutral features. It also bears on SAE assumptions (arguing dense/non-orthogonal SAE latents may reflect genuine multi-dimensional factor geometry rather than a flaw) and on the "platonic representation"/universal embedding literature, both cited explicitly as related work, making it a natural bridge for discussing whether multilingual interlingua-like subspaces could be an instance of this same factoring inductive bias.

### ReDeEP: Detecting Hallucination in Retrieval-Augmented Generation via Mechanistic Interpretability

**File:** `ReDeEP-2024-Detecting-Hallucination-in-RAG.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Sun, Zang, Zheng, Xu, Zhang, Yu, Song, Li — 2024/2025 (ICLR 2025)  
**Type:** paper

> Hallucinations in RAG models arise from a specific internal mechanism — Knowledge FFNs (late layers) over-injecting parametric knowledge into the residual stream while Copying Heads (attention heads) fail to retain retrieved external context — and decoupling these two signals via mechanistic-interpretability-derived scores gives a much better hallucination detector than existing methods.

**Problem:** RAG models can still hallucinate even when the retrieved context is accurate and relevant, because the model's output reflects some mixture of external (retrieved) knowledge and internal parametric knowledge, and these two sources can conflict. Existing hallucination-detection methods either look only at hidden states (letting external context confound the signal), only at output-vs-context comparison (letting parametric knowledge confound the signal), or mix both without disentangling their separate causal contributions — making detection inaccurate. The paper frames this as three flawed causal graphs and argues you need to decouple P (parametric knowledge) and E (external context) as separate covariates.

**Method:** Working on Llama2-7B/13B-Chat and Llama3-8B-Chat with the RAGTruth dataset, they define two per-token/per-response mechanistic scores using standard interpretability tools (residual stream decomposition, logit lens, attention circuits): (1) External Context Score (ECS) — cosine similarity between the last-layer hidden state of the generated token and the mean-pooled hidden states of the top-10% attended context tokens (identified via attention weights), measuring whether/how much an attention head's retrieved context content survives into the output; (2) Parametric Knowledge Score (PKS) — Jensen-Shannon divergence between the logit-lens vocabulary distributions before and after each FFN layer, measuring how much a given FFN adds to the residual stream (i.e., how much parametric knowledge it injects). Using correlation analysis and causal intervention (adding noise to attention scores of "Copying Heads" — heads identified by trace/eigenvalue analysis of the OV circuit as exhibiting copying behavior — vs. amplifying "Knowledge FFN" contributions to the residual stream, and measuring NLL differences), they establish that low ECS in Copying Heads and high PKS in late-layer Knowledge FFNs are causally linked to hallucination. They then build ReDeEP, a linear regression/detector that combines PKS (summed over Knowledge FFN layers) minus ECS (summed over Copying Heads) as a hallucination score, at both token-level and a more efficient chunk-level (using text chunking + BGE embeddings). They also propose AARF (Add Attention Reduce FFN), an inference-time intervention that amplifies Copying Head attention outputs and dampens Knowledge FFN outputs when the hallucination score exceeds a threshold, without updating model weights.

**Findings:** Empirically: 1006/1024 attention heads show higher ECS on truthful vs. hallucinated responses; ECS correlates negatively with hallucination and correlates with "Copying Head" identity (PCC ~0.41–0.46); late-layer FFN PKS is significantly higher for hallucinated responses (Pearson correlation up to ~0.4+ in later layers); causal interventions (noise on Copying Heads, 10x amplification of Knowledge FFNs) produce significantly larger NLL differences than matched control groups, confirming causal (not just correlational) roles. On detection benchmarks (RAGTruth and Dolly-AC, across three LLaMA backbones, measured by AUC/PCC/Recall/F1), ReDeEP (chunk-level variant especially) outperforms all baselines across three method categories (PCE, ECP, MPE — e.g., EigenScore/SEP/ITI, RAGAS/Trulens/ChainPoll, Perplexity/Energy/SelfCheckGPT), e.g. AUC 0.79–0.84 on several settings vs ~0.60–0.72 for best baselines. Ablations confirm both ECS and PKS are individually weaker than the combined regression, validating the decoupling/covariate approach. AARF intervention improves GPT-4o-judged truthfulness of generated responses relative to baseline decoding, with reasonable computational efficiency (chunk-level ReDeEP is among the cheapest detection methods, unlike LLM-judge baselines).

**Relevance:** This is a strong worked example of applying core mech-interp machinery — residual stream decomposition, logit lens, OV-circuit eigenvalue analysis to identify "copying heads," FFN-as-key-value-memory, causal intervention/ablation — to a concrete downstream behavior (hallucination) in a non-toy, production-scale LLM setting. For the interlingua-hypothesis seminar, it's most useful as a methodological template: how to operationalize "how much does component X contribute to token identity in the residual stream" via logit lens + JSD, and how to identify functional attention head types (copying heads) via QK/OV circuit analysis — techniques directly reusable for asking whether multilingual models have "copying/routing heads" or "language-specific vs. shared FFN knowledge stores" analogous to the parametric-vs-external decomposition here (e.g., decoupling language-specific surface encoding from a shared/interlingua semantic representation the way this paper decouples parametric vs. external knowledge use).

### FACTUM: Mechanistic Detection of Citation Hallucination in Long-Form RAG

**File:** `FACTUM-2026-Citation-Hallucination-in-LongForm-RAG.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Dassen, Kotula, Murray, Yates, Lawrie, Kayi, Mayfield, Duh (2026)  
**Venue:** arXiv preprint (arXiv:2601.05866v4), appears formatted for a peer-reviewed venue (LNCS-style, likely ECIR 2026 track given references to concurrent ECIR 2026 work)  
**Type:** paper

> Citation hallucination in long-form RAG is not simple over-reliance on parametric knowledge but a scale-dependent breakdown in how the Attention (reading) and FFN (recall) pathways coordinate, and this coordination signature can be mechanistically detected and shown to change from "agreement" at 3B scale to "specialized orthogonal contribution" at 8B scale.

**Problem:** RAG models frequently generate citations that misattribute claims to sources that don't actually support them ("citation hallucination"), which is especially dangerous because citations inflate reader trust even when unsupported, and the problem worsens in long-form, multi-document report generation due to "attribution drift" over long outputs. Prior mechanistic hallucination-detection work (notably ReDeEP) treats external-context and parametric-knowledge pathways as independent monolithic signals and uses imprecise proxies (whole-prompt attention diluted by system/query tokens; LogitLens vocabulary projections), leaving citation-specific hallucination detection largely unaddressed.

**Method:** The authors decompose the transformer residual stream update at a citation token into additive Attention and FFN contributions and define four mechanistic scores: (1) Contextual Alignment Score (CAS) — cosine similarity between the citation token's final-layer hidden state and an attention-weighted vector of source-document token hidden states, isolating grounding from only context tokens (unlike ReDeEP's whole-prompt ECS); (2) Beginning-of-Sentence Attention Score (BAS) — proportion of attention allocated to the <s> "attention sink" token, capturing information synthesis; (3) Parametric Force Score (PFS) — L2 norm of the FFN's additive update to the residual stream (pre- vs post-FFN state difference), a more direct alternative to LogitLens-based Parametric Knowledge Score; (4) Pathway Alignment Score (PAS) — cosine similarity between the attention update vector and FFN update vector, the first explicit geometric measure of pathway coordination (collaboration near +1, orthogonality near 0, conflict near -1). They evaluate on the TREC NeuCLIR 2024 long-form report-generation task (up to 15 machine-translated English documents per query) using Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (128k context), framing detection as token-level binary classification on the citation digit. Ground truth labels come from an LLM-as-judge (Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct via the ARGUE attestation framework), validated against human annotators (κ≈0.68 agreement). High-dimensional per-head/per-layer scores are pruned (top-k% components by training-set correlation, k=100% for 3B vs k=25% for 8B) and reduced via per-layer aggregation and time-series features (slope, FFT), yielding a compact 4-feature FACTUM model trained with Logistic Regression, LightGBM, and EBM classifiers under 10-fold report-level stratified cross-validation. Baselines include ReDeEP's ECS+PKS and confidence-based scores (Perplexity, LN-Entropy, Energy, P(True)).

**Findings:** FACTUM beats the ReDeEP baseline and confidence-based baselines across all three classifiers, winning 27/30 head-to-head comparisons, with peak AUC 0.737 (Logistic Regression, 8B) versus baseline AUC 0.536-0.613, and improving 8B precision from 0.201 to 0.334 (a 66% relative gain), up to 37.5% AUC improvement overall. Mechanistically, correct citations are consistently marked by higher PFS (parametric force) and higher BAS (attention-sink usage) across both models — directly contradicting the assumption that strong FFN/parametric activity signals hallucination; instead it appears constructive when coordinated with attention. However, the coordination signature is scale-dependent: the 3B model (using 100% of components) shows correct citations have uniformly higher CAS, BAS, PAS, and PFS — a "direct reinforcement" strategy where all pathways agree. The 8B model (optimal at only top-25% of pruned components) shows a shift to "specialized contribution": CAS becomes inconsistent/reversed and PAS becomes significantly lower for correct citations under the best classifier, meaning pathways contribute distinct, orthogonal (not redundant) information. A case study on their motivating example shows hallucinated citations exhibit "mechanistic dissonance": high sustained PFS (the FFN pushing its memorized "100mg" fact) combined with low CAS and decreased BAS — i.e., the FFN pathway wins out over grounded context integration, independent of model scale.

**Relevance:** Directly relevant as a case study in decomposing transformer internals (attention vs. FFN pathways) into interpretable, causally-motivated scores tied to the residual stream, and in showing that the "correct" internal coordination signature is not fixed but shifts qualitatively with scale (redundant agreement at small scale vs. specialized/orthogonal division of labor at larger scale) — a useful analog for discussions of whether shared/interlingua-like representations in multilingual models might similarly reorganize from redundant to specialized coding as scale increases. Also demonstrates a general methodological template (pathway decomposition, geometric alignment/cosine-similarity probes, per-head/per-layer pruning) that could be adapted to probe whether language-specific vs. language-neutral information is carried more by attention or FFN pathways in multilingual settings.

### Humans and transformer LMs: Abstraction drives language learning

**File:** `EACL-2026-long-32.pdf`  
**Authors/Year:** Jian and Manning, 2026  
**Type:** paper

> Tracking GPT-2's next-token predictions across ~450 training checkpoints shows that class-level (abstract) linguistic generalizations emerge before and drive item-specific (lexical) learning, supporting an abstraction-first rather than exemplar-first account of how LMs (and possibly children) acquire language.

**Problem:** Two competing theories of human language acquisition disagree about developmental order: the abstraction-first account (children learn abstract featural/structural categories early, e.g. verb classes, and generalize downward to items) vs. the exemplar-first account (children memorize specific lexical item-context pairings first and abstractions emerge gradually via generalization over stored exemplars). The paper asks which account better characterizes how a standard transformer LM (GPT-2) actually learns linguistic categories over the course of pretraining, using this as a case study in what LMs can serve as an "existence proof" for regarding human cognition.

**Method:** Using 450 GPT-2-small (12-layer, 12-head) training checkpoints spanning 400K steps (Karamcheti et al. 2021 checkpoints, trained on OpenWebText), the authors track next-token prediction distributions at a fixed target slot across two experiments. Experiment 1 studies argument structure: four verb classes (to-Dative, Motion, Reciprocal, spray-load verbs; sentences filtered from WikiText-103 via regex + Stanza parses) are compared using pairwise Jensen-Shannon divergence (D_JS) between verb-level averaged next-token distributions. An "item learning metric" tracks in-class divergence (verb-specific idiosyncrasy) and a "class learning metric" uses a one-tailed Mann-Whitney U-test comparing within-class vs. between-class D_JS to detect when the model systematically distinguishes classes. An exemplar-first baseline is built from co-occurrence count vectors (with add-k smoothing) as a contrast condition, using token count as a proxy for training progress. Experiment 2 extends the same divergence/U-test paradigm to two syntactic phenomena: TRANSITIVITY (transitive/intransitive subcategorization, from BLiMP) and RELATIVE CLAUSE filler-gap dependencies (comparing relative-clause vs. matrix-clause environments for the same verbs), to test whether abstraction-first learning generalizes beyond semantic verb classes to syntax.

**Findings:** Across all four verb classes and both syntactic phenomena, statistically significant between-class differentiation (class-general behavior) emerges at or before item-specific (in-class) divergence — i.e., as soon as any behavioral change is detected, it already reflects abstract category-level distinctions rather than idiosyncratic per-verb learning; for to-Dative/Motion, class differentiation actually precedes item differentiation by ~50 steps. The count-based exemplar-first baseline shows the opposite pattern (verb-specific divergence first, driven by idiosyncratic co-occurrence, converging into class structure only later), confirming the GPT-2 pattern isn't an artifact of the training data. Different abstractions are learned sequentially and abruptly (discrete "onsets," not gradual): TRANSITIVITY earliest (t<100), semantic verb-argument-structure classes next (t>100), and non-local relative-clause dependencies last (t>1000), consistent across three random seeds and roughly matching typical child language-development ordering. The authors conclude LMs exhibit an abstraction-first bias, but caution that unlike proposed innate human abstractions, LM abstractions are learned purely distributionally from data.

**Relevance:** Only tangentially relevant to the interlingua hypothesis — this paper is about a single English monolingual GPT-2 model and does not address multilinguality or shared cross-language representations at all. Its relevance to a mech-interp/multilingual seminar is methodological: it offers a divergence-based (Jensen-Shannon), checkpoint-trajectory paradigm for detecting when and how abstract categories emerge during training, which could be adapted to ask analogous questions about multilingual models (e.g., whether language-neutral/shared representations emerge before or after language-specific ones), and it exemplifies careful controls (exemplar baseline) for distinguishing genuine representational abstraction from surface statistical artifacts — a methodological template relevant to testing the interlingua hypothesis empirically.
