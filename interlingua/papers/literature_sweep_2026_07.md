# Positioning Report: Cross-Lingual/Cross-Modal Convergence, Reference-Free MT Quality, and Production Dubbing for African Low-Resource Languages

## 1. State of the field in 2026

**Background term:** the "interlingua hypothesis" is the old idea (from the mBERT/LASER era, roughly 2018-2022) that multilingual models learn one single, language-neutral "universal meaning space" — like an internal Esperanto that every language gets translated into and out of. Nobody believes the strong version of this anymore. The question in 2026 is what actually replaced it.

**A. Do models share internal representations across languages? Mostly yes, but it's messier than "one interlingua."** The anchor result is *Do Llamas Work in English?* (Wendler et al., ACL 2024), which showed Llama-2 processes non-English prompts by passing through an English-biased "concept space" in its middle layers before producing output in the input language — English acts as an implicit pivot. This was generalized by *The Semantic Hub Hypothesis* (Wu et al., ICLR 2025), which showed the same convergence phenomenon extends beyond language to modality (code, math, images, audio): semantically equivalent inputs from different languages and data types converge in a shared intermediate layer, and — importantly — you can intervene on that shared space and the effect propagates across languages and modalities, meaning it's not just a coincidence of geometry, it's doing real work. Anthropic's *On the Biology of a Large Language Model* (2025) found similar mixed language-specific/language-independent circuitry inside Claude 3.5 Haiku, with the shared-feature fraction growing as models get bigger.

But the "English is always the pivot" story turned out to be an artifact of *unbalanced* training data, not a law of transformers. *What Language(s) Does Aya-23 Think In?* (Trinley et al., 2025) found that a model trained on genuinely balanced multilingual data uses typologically-related languages as local pivots instead of always routing through English. This matters a lot for the student's project because the lab's NLLB models were presumably trained on real parallel data for each African language, not English-dominated web text — so the "hub" language, if one exists at all, might not be English.

**B. Representation quality and output quality are turning out to be separate questions.** Several 2025 papers (*The Translation Barrier Hypothesis*, Bafna et al.; the cross-layer-transcoder tracing paper, Harrasse et al.) now argue that models "understand" low-resource-language content about as well internally as high-resource content — the shared conceptual representation is there — but they fail specifically at the last step of *translating that correct internal understanding back into fluent target-language text* (the "decode-out" step). This is a genuinely important reframing: it means a metric that measures how close two representations are (like an embedding-distance quality signal) is testing something different from "did the model produce a good translation." The two can disagree, and low-resource languages are exactly where they're most likely to.

**C. Localizing "language-specific neurons" causally is harder than it looked.** *Language-Specific Neurons* (Tang et al., ACL 2024) found sparse neurons in feedforward layers that seem to control which language a model outputs. But *Language-specific Neurons Do Not Facilitate Cross-Lingual Transfer* (Mondal et al., 2025) showed that fine-tuning specifically on those neurons doesn't actually improve cross-lingual performance — a genuine negative result. This is a caution against over-interpreting "I found a shared/separate circuit" as proof that circuit causes good behavior. Newer work (*Transfer Neurons Hypothesis*, 2025) tries to patch this by proposing more causally-grounded "routing" neurons instead.

**D. The "Platonic Representation Hypothesis" (the general theory underneath all of this) is being pushed back on hard, right now, in 2026.** Huh et al.'s original paper (ICML 2024) argued that as models scale and diversify, their internal geometries converge toward a shared statistical model of reality. Two 2026 papers directly attack the methodology behind that claim: *Revisiting the Platonic Representation Hypothesis: An Aristotelian View* (Gröger et al.) shows the standard similarity metrics (CKA, mutual-nearest-neighbor) are confounded by model width and depth — wider models look more "similar" even when representations are actually independent — and once you use a statistically calibrated (permutation-null) test, most of the claimed *global* convergence disappears; only *local* neighborhood structure ("who is near whom") survives. *Back into Plato's Cave* (2026) shows separately that measured convergence collapses when you scale the evaluation set from ~1,000 samples to millions — meaning many published convergence numbers may be artifacts of small test sets. **Takeaway for the student: any convergence number this project reports needs a properly calibrated metric and a scale-robustness check, or reviewers will not trust it.**

**E. Speech-vs-text convergence (the "modality" half of the student's question) is a real but thin and young literature, and results are more pessimistic than the language-only story.** *How do Multimodal Foundation Models Encode Text and Speech?* (Lee et al., NAACL 2025) is the closest existing study to what the student wants to do — it measures text-vs-speech convergence inside multimodal models across many languages, including a few low-resource ones (Amharic among them). Findings: convergence generally increases with depth, but modality differences can be *larger* than language differences (speech-in-language-A can be more different from text-in-language-A than text-in-language-A is from text-in-language-B). Speech representations show more cross-lingual variance than text. And critically: the field's current fixes for this gap only work for high-resource languages — an explicit, citable open problem. *Interleaved Speech Language Models Latently Work In Text* (Sternberg et al., 2026) adds a mechanistic angle: speech-based language models implicitly transcribe speech to text internally before generating, a speech-modality analogue of the English-pivot finding — suggesting text may function as an implicit "pivot modality" much like English functions as a pivot language.

**F. There is a whole production infrastructure already built around the idea of a shared speech+text multilingual space, called SONAR.** Meta's SONAR (2023), extended by BLASER 2.0 (2024) and now Omnilingual SONAR (2026), is a single fixed-size sentence embedding space — initialized from NLLB's own text encoder — into which speech from many languages is also mapped via a teacher-student distillation process. This is essentially an *engineered* version of the interlingua idea: convergence isn't discovered, it's trained in on purpose. BLASER 2.0 already provides a reference-free quality-estimation (QE) score based on cosine distance between source and output SONAR embeddings — extremely close in spirit to what the student is proposing to build for part B. Its speech-language coverage (57 languages) and African-language depth are largely unverified in independent benchmarks, which is a real opening.

**G. Reference-free MT quality estimation (QE) is a mature, settled architecture pattern, but is documented to be unreliable for the lowest-resource languages, including many African ones.** The standard recipe (encode source + hypothesis with a multilingual encoder, either regress to human judgment scores or take embedding cosine distance) underlies COMET/CometKiwi, MetricX, AfriCOMET, and BLASER 2.0. The WMT Metrics/QE shared tasks are the field's benchmark venue. As of WMT25 (*Findings of the WMT25 Shared Task*), the organizers found that linguistic diversity — not which modeling approach you use — is the dominant bottleneck, and for the very lowest-resource test languages (Bhojpuri, Maasai) they gave up on QE metrics entirely and fell back to the older surface metric chrF++. AfriCOMET (Wang et al., NAACL 2024) is the leading African-specific QE model — it substantially improves correlation with human judgment over generic COMET/CometKiwi by swapping in an African-centric encoder (AfroXLM-R), but even its own authors report weak correlation (~0.44 Spearman) for the hardest pairs (English-Luo, English-Twi), and it only covers 13 African languages — a fraction of "dozens." Known failure modes documented in 2025 work include systematic length bias (QE metrics over-penalize longer outputs regardless of correctness — directly relevant to dubbing, where translations are squeezed/stretched for lip-sync timing) and unreliable behavior when the encoder's pretraining barely covers a language (LASER/LaBSE-based sentence alignment has been shown to degrade badly on African languages like Luhya that are underrepresented in pretraining).

**H. The African-language MT/TTS landscape has strong foundation models but uneven, hard-to-trust evaluation.** NLLB-200, MADLAD-400, Meta's MMS (speech, largely Bible-derived training data), and SeamlessM4T are the standard backbones. FLORES-200, the standard MT benchmark, has documented translation errors in several African-language splits (Hausa, Sepedi, Xitsonga, isiZulu — *Correcting FLORES Evaluation Dataset for Four African Languages*, WMT 2024), so scores should not be trusted at face value. TTS evaluation for African languages is younger and more fragmented than MT evaluation; there's no FLORES-equivalent standard benchmark. The methodological convergence that is happening is on using ASR round-trip word error rate (feed TTS output back through a speech recognizer and check if you recover the original text) as an automatic intelligibility proxy, with newer work (*PashtoTTS-Bench*, 2026) warning that plain ASR-WER can be silently wrong if the ASR model hallucinates in the wrong script or language, and proposing an added script-fidelity/language-ID check.

## 2. Novelty verdict

**Short answer: yes, the project is still open territory, but only if it's scoped and positioned carefully. Each of the three pieces has close, recent precedent — nobody has done the specific combination.**

Going piece by piece:

- **Part C (cross-language x cross-modality convergence)**: The exact question — "for the *same* content, does a model's internal representation converge across BOTH language and modality, specifically for African low-resource languages, using a lab's own separately-trained MT and TTS models" — was not found as an existing paper. But three papers come close enough that the student must explicitly cite and differentiate from them:
  - *The Semantic Hub Hypothesis* (2024) established the general claim (convergence across language + modality) but didn't test African languages, didn't use dedicated speech-text parallel data, and didn't use production MT/TTS models.
  - *How do Multimodal Foundation Models Encode Text and Speech?* (Lee et al., NAACL 2025) is the closest existing paper — it asks almost exactly this question — but on general multimodal foundation models (SeamlessM4T, SONAR, SALMONN), not on a lab's own NLLB+XTTS pair, and its low-resource-language coverage is thin (mostly mid-resource languages, with Amharic as the closest African example). The authors themselves flag that their fixes for the modality gap only work for high-resource languages.
  - SONAR/Omnilingual SONAR are, in effect, an *engineered* version of the same convergence the student wants to *discover*. This is the single most important thing to grapple with: SONAR forces text and speech into the same space via training objective (distillation against a frozen NLLB-derived text target). NLLB and XTTS in the lab's setup were **not** trained to share a space — they were trained independently. So the interesting, non-redundant question is not "can you build a shared space" (SONAR already proved you can) but "does convergence emerge *without being trained for it*, in models that were never told to align." That's a different, less-asked question and is the strongest angle to lean into.
  - A separate close hit, *Universal Conceptual Structure: Probing NLLB-200's Multilingual Geometry* (2026), directly probes NLLB internals and finds a language-neutral "conceptual store" plus language-specific offsets across 135 languages — this is genuinely close to part of what the student wants to do on the text side. But it's text-only (no speech/modality dimension at all), uses static word-list probing (Swadesh lists) rather than natural sentence data, and is purely geometric analysis (no SAEs, no circuits, no interventions) — leaving room for a more mechanistic, sentence-level, and modality-extended follow-up.

- **Part B (reference-free QE via embedding distance)**: This is **not a novel technique in the abstract**. Embedding-distance-based reference-free QE (source-vs-output cosine similarity) is a well-established family (round-trip translation with sentence embeddings, SentSim, PRISM, COMET-QE, BLASER-QE) and has already been applied specifically to African languages — a 2024 storyboard-translation paper used LASER cosine similarity across Hausa/Swahili/Yoruba, and a 2022 paper (Akera et al.) tested LASER/LaBSE thresholds for aligning Luhya-Swahili sentence pairs, finding both degrade badly on languages absent from pretraining. **What's missing is deployment**: nobody has wired this signal into a live, production dubbing pipeline as an operational quality gate for African-language MT+TTS output, and nobody has systematically validated/calibrated it per-language for a set of African languages this broad, accounting for uneven encoder pretraining coverage.

- **Part A (production deployment)**: Amazon Science has already published on production automatic-dubbing architecture (ASR→MT→prosody alignment→TTS, verbosity control, isochrony), so the pipeline-architecture question isn't novel — but none of that work uses an embedding-distance semantic QE signal as the control/monitoring layer, and there is essentially no published work on cost-efficient AWS serving specifically for African-language NLLB+XTTS pairs. This is genuinely open, mostly because it's an engineering problem nobody has bothered to publish rather than because it's intellectually hard.

**What must be adjusted to avoid overlap:**
1. Explicitly position against SONAR/Omnilingual SONAR and the Semantic Hub Hypothesis papers as the closest prior art for part C, and frame the contribution as "does convergence emerge *without* explicit training for it" rather than "can convergence be engineered" (already answered, by SONAR).
2. Do not present embedding-distance QE as a novel *method* — present it as a novel *validation study + deployment* for African languages specifically, explicitly citing AfriCOMET/AfriMTE as the existing bar and BLASER-QE as the existing modality-agnostic baseline to compare against or build on.
3. Use a *calibrated* convergence metric (see Gröger et al.'s permutation-null correction) and test scale-robustness (per *Back into Plato's Cave*) — otherwise a reviewer familiar with 2026 critiques of the Platonic Representation Hypothesis will discount any "we found convergence" claim.
4. The genuinely freshest angle, not claimed by anyone in the sweep, is **connecting parts B and C causally**: using measured representational convergence/divergence to *explain* why the QE signal works well for some African languages and fails for others (hypothesis: languages poorly represented in encoder pretraining show both weak convergence AND unreliable QE scores). Nobody has made this link. It would turn three separate deliverables into one coherent thesis narrative.

## 3. What this changes about the project

**Encoder/metric choices:**
- For the QE signal (part B), **do not build from raw LASER/generic mE5 embeddings** — the literature (Akera et al., 2022; general LASER/LaBSE African-language findings) shows these degrade badly on languages absent from pretraining, exactly the failure mode the student's languages are most at risk of. Instead:
  - Benchmark against **AfriCOMET/AfriMTE** as the existing supervised baseline (13 languages, so likely a partial overlap with the lab's set) and **AfriE5** (from *AfriMTEB and AfriE5*, 2025/2026) as a sentence encoder specifically validated on ~59 African languages — this is probably the single best off-the-shelf encoder to build the embedding-distance signal on top of, rather than assuming a generic multilingual encoder generalizes.
  - Also benchmark **BLASER 2.0 / SONAR embeddings** directly, since they're the only existing reference-free metric that natively spans both speech and text — this gives a natural comparison point for the dubbing use case (source speech vs. TTS output, not just source text vs. MT output).
  - Before trusting any encoder on the lab's specific languages, run a coverage audit: check whether each African language was actually in the encoder's pretraining data and how much. The literature has no such audit; producing one is itself a small, useful contribution.

- For convergence (part C), use **middle-layer** representations, not final-layer — nearly every paper in the sweep (Wendler et al., Liu & Niehues, Lee et al.) finds convergence peaks in middle layers and degrades toward input/output layers. Consider the SAE-based approach (*Sparse Autoencoders Can Capture Language-Specific Concepts*, 2025; *Universal Sparse Autoencoders*, 2025) as a more mechanistic alternative to raw distance metrics — this is also the area the novelty-check sweep flags as most underexplored for encoder-decoder MT models like NLLB specifically (most "NLLB interpretability" so far is geometric probing, not SAE/circuit-level).

**Interesting comparisons to run:**
1. Does convergence (language-only, text-to-text) look different for the lab's NLLB models — trained on real parallel data — versus the English-pivot pattern found in Llama-family models trained on English-heavy web text? (Tests the Trinley et al. "balanced data removes the English bias" hypothesis directly, with a model class nobody has checked yet.)
2. Within the 2x2 grid (English vs. African language) x (speech vs. text), which axis shows a bigger gap — language or modality? Lee et al. found modality can dominate language on their (mostly non-African) sample; does that replicate, worsen, or improve on truly low-resource African data?
3. Does QE-signal reliability per language correlate with representational-convergence strength per language? (The novel connective contribution nobody has made — ties B and C together.)
4. Does convergence strength correlate with the amount of parallel data available per language (the lab has variable amounts of parallel data across its "dozens" of languages) — this is a natural, data-driven story the lab's setup is unusually well positioned to test, since most papers use a fixed model and can't vary training data volume per language this way.

**Pitfalls the literature explicitly warns about:**
- **Do not report raw CKA/mutual-nearest-neighbor convergence numbers without a calibrated null baseline** — Gröger et al. show these are confounded by model width/depth and inflate similarity even for independent representations.
- **Do not evaluate convergence on a small gallery/sample size** — Back into Plato's Cave shows apparent convergence can collapse when you scale from ~1K to millions of examples; report results at multiple sample sizes.
- **Representation similarity is not the same as output quality** — the Translation Barrier Hypothesis and cross-layer-transcoder tracing work argue the low-resource bottleneck is in decode-out, not understanding, so a high embedding-distance similarity score doesn't guarantee good translation and vice versa; don't conflate the two claims in the writeup.
- **Length bias**: QE metrics systematically penalize/reward by output length independent of correctness (Zhang et al., 2025) — dubbing translations are deliberately length-constrained for lip-sync, so this bias could badly confound the QE signal specifically in this application. Control for length explicitly.
- **Localizing a "language-specific" circuit/neuron does not mean it's causally load-bearing** — the Mondal et al. negative result on LAPE neurons is a direct warning against over-claiming causal importance from correlational probing.
- **FLORES-200 African-language splits contain real translation errors** — don't treat FLORES scores as ground truth for the lab's target languages without checking against the corrected splits or the lab's own data.
- **TTS quality is highly domain-sensitive** — most open African TTS resources are Bible-derived; if the lab's XTTS training data is different in domain (dubbing = conversational/entertainment content), don't assume published TTS benchmark numbers transfer.

## 4. Top 12 reading list

1. **How do Multimodal Foundation Models Encode Text and Speech? An Analysis of Cross-Lingual and Cross-Modal Representations** (Lee, Liu, Sinhamahapatra, Niehues; NAACL 2025) — the single closest existing study to the project's core research question (part C); read first to understand exactly what's already been shown and where it stops (high-resource languages only).

2. **The Semantic Hub Hypothesis: Language Models Share Semantic Representations Across Languages and Modalities** (Wu et al., ICLR 2025) — the modern theoretical anchor for "interlingua across language AND modality," including causal intervention evidence; the paper to position the whole thesis against.

3. **SONAR: Sentence-Level Multimodal and Language-Agnostic Representations** (Duquenne et al., 2023) plus **BLASER 2.0** (Dale & Costa-jussà, EMNLP Findings 2024) — read together; this is the production system that already engineers the shared text+speech space the student wants to study, and BLASER-QE is the nearest existing baseline for part B.

4. **Do Llamas Work in English? On the Latent Language of Multilingual Transformers** (Wendler et al., ACL 2024) — the foundational "English pivot" result and the methodology (logit lens) the student can adapt to test whether the lab's NLLB models pivot through English or not.

5. **Revisiting the Platonic Representation Hypothesis: An Aristotelian View** (Gröger, Wen & Brbić, 2026) — essential methodological corrective; read before designing any convergence metric, since it shows naive similarity scores are statistically confounded.

6. **AfriMTE and AfriCOMET: Enhancing COMET to Embrace Under-resourced African Languages** (Wang, Adelani et al., NAACL 2024) — the existing African-specific QE baseline the student's part B must be benchmarked against; also documents exactly which language pairs remain hardest.

7. **AfriMTEB and AfriE5: Benchmarking and Adapting Text Embedding Models for African Languages** (2025/2026) — the best candidate off-the-shelf sentence encoder for building the embedding-distance QE signal, pre-validated on ~59 African languages rather than assumed to generalize.

8. **The Translation Barrier Hypothesis: Multilingual Generation with LLMs Suffers from Implicit Translation Failure** (Bafna et al., 2025) — reframes low-resource MT failure as a "decode-out" problem rather than an "understanding" problem; critical for correctly interpreting what the convergence study can and can't tell you about output quality.

9. **Universal Conceptual Structure in Neural Translation: Probing NLLB-200's Multilingual Geometry** (2026) — the closest existing NLLB-internals study; shows a language-neutral conceptual subspace plus language-specific offsets, but text-only and non-mechanistic — the direct gap to extend.

10. **Findings of the WMT25 Shared Task on Automated Translation Evaluation Systems** (Lavie et al., 2025) — the most current statement of the field's ceiling on low-resource QE reliability, including the explicit admission that QE was abandoned for the very lowest-resource test languages; sets realistic expectations for part B.

11. **Back into Plato's Cave: Examining Cross-modal Representational Convergence at Scale** (2026) — a sharp methodological warning that convergence claims are highly sensitive to evaluation-sample size; needed to make part C's results credible.

12. **Correcting FLORES Evaluation Dataset for Four African Languages** (Abdulmumin et al., WMT 2024) — short but important practical read; a direct warning not to trust standard African-language benchmark scores at face value when evaluating the lab's NLLB provider.
